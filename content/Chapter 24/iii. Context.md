## The Exodus Pattern Behind the Trumpets

The plagues of Egypt are the model for the trumpet judgments, and the correspondences are close enough to be deliberate. The first trumpet's hail and fire recalls the seventh plague, when hail fell with fire flashing back and forth (Exodus 9:22–26). The second and third trumpets recall the first plague, when the Nile turned to blood, its fish died, and its water became undrinkable (Exodus 7:20–21). The fourth trumpet recalls the ninth plague, a darkness over the land that could be felt (Exodus 10:21–23). The fifth trumpet's locusts recall the eighth plague (Exodus 10:12–15). The order differs from Exodus, and the scale changes: in Egypt the plagues fell on a single nation, while the trumpets strike a third of the earth. But the pattern of a judgment falling on one people while another is set apart is present in both accounts.

That distinction is stated explicitly in Exodus. The flies do not enter the region of Goshen, where Israel lives (Exodus 8:22–23). No hail falls in Goshen (Exodus 9:26). During the darkness, all the Israelites have light in the places where they live (Exodus 10:23). The same logic drives the sealing of the 144,000, protected from what the locusts are released to do, and the same distinction between a target and a watching audience.

The plagues also carried a stated purpose: that Egypt would know that he is the Lord (Exodus 7:5, 17; see also 9:14, 16). The Exodus judgments were designed to produce knowledge of who God is, in Egypt and in Israel. The trumpets aim at the same result on a larger stage, for an audience that does not yet know who God is.

## Wormwood: The Bitter Water of the Prophets

Two backgrounds converge on the third trumpet, and they contribute different things. The scene comes from the Exodus, and the name comes from the prophets.

The scene is Marah. Israel arrives at water in the wilderness and cannot drink it because it is bitter; the place is named Marah, which means bitter. God shows Moses a piece of wood, Moses throws it into the water, and the water becomes fit to drink (Exodus 15:22–25). The text does not name the wood and does not call it wormwood. What the Marah story supplies is the pattern: bitter water is a threat to the life of a people in a dry land, and God is the one who can turn it. The third trumpet is that pattern in reverse. Water is embittered rather than sweetened, and it kills.

The name comes from a Hebrew word, *la'anah*, translated in the Septuagint by the Greek *apsinthos*, which is the name John gives the star in Revelation 8:11. The word denotes a bitter plant and is the prophets' proverbial image of bitterness and poison. Jeremiah uses it as an instrument of judgment on a covenant-breaking people: the Lord says he will feed them wormwood and give them poisoned water to drink (Jeremiah 9:15; 23:15). Lamentations uses it for the experience of bitterness under judgment (Lamentations 3:15, 19), and Amos accuses Israel of turning justice into wormwood (Amos 5:7; 6:12). Deuteronomy 29:18 warns against a root that produces bitter poison among the people.

Read together, the two backgrounds explain the choice of name. Marah supplies the scene of bitter water. The prophets supply the meaning of the word: judgment, poison, the consequences of forsaking the God who provides. Revelation compresses both into a single name.

## Locusts in Scripture: Exodus and Joel

Locusts are among the most feared agents of devastation in the ancient Near East. The eighth plague of Exodus describes a swarm so dense that it covers the ground until it is black and strips every plant and all the fruit on the trees (Exodus 10:12–15). Clouds that block the sun and consume everything green make up the standard biblical picture.

The prophet Joel takes the locust swarm and turns it into an image of the Day of the Lord. His locust army has the teeth of a lion (Joel 1:6). It has the appearance of horses and gallops like cavalry (Joel 2:4), with a sound like chariots leaping over the mountaintops (Joel 2:5). Before it, the earth shakes, the sky trembles, and the sun and moon are darkened (Joel 2:10). Nearly every element of John's description in Revelation 9:7–9 has an antecedent in Joel: horses prepared for battle, lions' teeth, the sound of chariots.

Where Revelation departs from both Exodus and Joel is instructive. These locusts are told not to harm the grass, any plant, or any tree. Ordinary locusts cannot be told anything, and they eat whatever is green. The instructions given to Revelation's locusts, and the limit placed on them to torment without killing, mark them as beings that hear commands and obey them. John uses the language of the plague tradition to describe something that is not a plague of insects.

## The Abyss and the Prisons of the Unseen World

The Greek word *abyssos* means bottomless or unfathomable depth. In the Septuagint it translates the Hebrew *tehom*, the deep of Genesis 1:2, which connects the abyss to the Bible's older imagery of chaotic depths. In the New Testament it is a place of confinement for spiritual beings. The demons in Luke 8:31 beg Jesus not to send them into it, and Revelation 9 shows it opened.

Scripture uses several terms for confinement and judgment, and they overlap in imagery: darkness, restraint, separation from God. That overlap is why the concepts tend to run together, but the terms are not interchangeable. Second Peter 2:4 uses the verb form of Tartarus, found nowhere else in the New Testament, for the confinement of angels who sinned, held in chains of darkness for judgment, and it is closely tied to the imprisonment of the Watchers. Hades is the realm of the dead. The lake of fire is reserved for the final judgment. The abyss is the holding place from which, at the fifth trumpet, its inhabitants are briefly released.

The Second Temple sources fill in the background the New Testament assumes. In 1 Enoch, the leading Watchers are bound in darkness until the day of judgment (1 Enoch 10), while the spirits of the Nephilim, the demons, continue to roam the earth (1 Enoch 15–16). Jubilees 10 records the division cited in the chapter: after Noah prays for the demons to be shut away, the chief of spirits Mastema intercedes, and God confines nine-tenths of them while leaving a tenth under Mastema's authority. That tradition helps explain why the demons in Matthew 8:29 speak of being tormented "before the appointed time." They know a scheduled judgment is coming.

## Stars, Angels, and the Fallen Star

The identification of the fifth trumpet's star as an angel rests on a consistent biblical pattern. In Job 38:7 the morning stars sing together in parallel with the sons of God shouting for joy, treating the two as the same company. In Revelation 1:20 the seven stars are explicitly identified as angels. A star that receives a key and opens a shaft, in a book that elsewhere uses stars for angelic beings, is an angel.

A textual note affects how the eagle of Revelation 8:13 appears in different translations. The earliest and best manuscripts read "eagle." A smaller group of later manuscripts, followed by the King James Version, reads "angel." Readers using the KJV will find an angel flying in midair; most modern translations read eagle.

## Abaddon

The Hebrew word *abaddon* means destruction. In the Hebrew Bible it appears as a name for the realm of the dead, paired with Sheol and with death: *Destruction* lies uncovered before God (Job 26:6), and Death and Destruction lie open before the Lord (Proverbs 15:11; see also Job 28:22 and Psalm 88:11). In Revelation 9:11 the word becomes a person. The angel of the abyss, king over the locust army, bears the name in Hebrew, and in Greek he is called Apollyon, the Destroyer. John gives the name in both languages.
