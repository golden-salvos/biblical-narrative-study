## Hallelujah

*Hallelujah* is a Hebrew imperative: *hallelu*, "praise," and *Yah*, the short form of the divine name [[Glossary#Yahweh|Yahweh]]. It means "Praise the Lord." The word is common in the Psalms, where it opens or closes many of the psalms from 104 to 150, and it gives its name to the *Hallel*, Psalms 113–118, which Israel sang at the great feasts. At Passover the Hallel was sung in two parts during the meal, and the hymn Jesus and his disciples sang before going out to the Mount of Olives (Matthew 26:30) is traditionally understood to be its closing psalms.

In the entire New Testament, the word appears only in Revelation 19:1–6, four times in six verses. The first great Hallelujah of the church in heaven answers the fall of Babylon; the last answers the announcement that the Lord God Almighty reigns and the marriage of the Lamb has come. The smoke from Babylon going up "forever and ever" (Revelation 19:3) repeats Isaiah's oracle against Edom, whose smoke "shall go up forever" (Isaiah 34:10).

## The Saints as Rulers in Place of the Fallen Powers

The claim that believers take the place of the rebellious members of the divine council has a firm footing in Scripture. Psalm 82 pictures God standing in the council of the gods and sentencing them for unjust rule over the nations: *"You are gods, sons of the Most High, all of you; nevertheless, like men you shall die, and fall like any prince"* (Psalm 82:6–7). Daniel 7 describes the transfer that follows. After the beasts are judged, *"the kingdom and the dominion and the greatness of the kingdoms under the whole heaven shall be given to the people of the saints of the Most High"* (Daniel 7:27).

The New Testament makes the same move explicitly. Hebrews 2:5 states that *"it was not to angels that God subjected the world to come."* Paul tells the Corinthians that the saints will judge the world and even angels (1 Corinthians 6:2–3). Christ promises the overcomer at Thyatira authority over the nations, to rule them with a rod of iron (Revelation 2:26–27), and the overcomer at Laodicea a seat with him on his throne (Revelation 3:21). The redeemed of Revelation 5:10 are made *"a kingdom and priests to our God, and they shall reign on the earth."* Michael Heiser's work on the divine council, especially *The Unseen Realm*, develops this reading at length: the human family of God displaces the disloyal sons of God who ruled the nations after Babel.

## Bride and Bridegroom

The marriage of the Lamb draws on a picture that runs through the whole Bible. In the prophets, God is Israel's husband. *"For your Maker is your husband, the Lord of hosts is his name"* (Isaiah 54:5). *"As the bridegroom rejoices over the bride, so shall your God rejoice over you"* (Isaiah 62:5). Hosea's marriage to an unfaithful wife becomes a living parable of Israel's idolatry and God's promise to betroth her to himself forever, *"in righteousness and in justice, in steadfast love and in mercy"* (Hosea 2:19–20). Idolatry is consistently described as adultery.

Jesus calls himself the bridegroom (Mark 2:19–20), and John the Baptist calls himself the friend of the bridegroom (John 3:29). Paul describes betrothing the Corinthian church *"to one husband, to present you as a pure virgin to Christ"* (2 Corinthians 11:2), and he presents Christ's love for the church as the pattern for marriage, giving himself up for her *"so that he might present the church to himself in splendor, without spot or wrinkle"* (Ephesians 5:25–27). The Bride of Revelation 19 appears again as the New Jerusalem, *"prepared as a bride adorned for her husband"* (Revelation 21:2, 9–10).

### Jewish Wedding Customs

A Jewish wedding in the first century came in two stages. The first was the betrothal (*erusin* or *kiddushin*), a legally binding commitment made in front of witnesses, often sealed with a bride-price; breaking it required a divorce, which is why Joseph considered divorcing Mary while they were only betrothed (Matthew 1:18–19). The couple did not yet live together. Some time later, often about a year, the groom came to bring the bride to his home, usually in a procession at night with torches and lamps. The wedding feast followed and could last up to a week (Judges 14:12; Genesis 29:27).

That shape is visible in the New Testament: Jesus tells his disciples he is going to prepare a place for them and will come again to take them to himself (John 14:2–3), and the ten virgins wait at night with lamps for the bridegroom's arrival. Many popular sermons fill in this outline with additional detail, such as the groom building a room onto his father's house and the father alone deciding when it is finished. Those details are drawn mostly from later rabbinic sources and from modern reconstruction, and the first-century evidence for them is thinner than it is often presented. The two-stage pattern itself is well established.

## Wedding Garments and Righteousness

The parable of the wedding banquet (Matthew 22:1–14) turns on a guest without a wedding garment. Interpreters have long asked why a man called in off the road would be expected to have one. A common explanation is that the host supplied garments for his guests, which would make refusing to wear one an insult. There is some support for kings giving robes to guests or servants (Genesis 45:22; 2 Kings 10:22), but no clear evidence that this was a general wedding custom, so the explanation should be held lightly. The parable itself does not explain the garment, which is why Revelation 19:8 is so useful in reading it.

The Old Testament uses clothing for righteousness in several places. Isaiah 61:10 rejoices that God *"has clothed me with the garments of salvation; he has covered me with the robe of righteousness, as a bridegroom decks himself... and as a bride adorns herself with her jewels."* In Zechariah 3:3–5, Joshua the high priest stands before the angel of the Lord in filthy garments, which are removed and replaced with pure vestments as a sign that his iniquity has been taken away.

The Greek of Revelation 19:8 is precise. The word for "righteous deeds" is *dikaiōmata*, a plural meaning righteous acts, not the abstract quality of righteousness. Yet the verse also says the linen *"was granted"* to the Bride (*edothē*, a divine passive, given by God). Interpreters weigh the two halves differently. Some emphasize the gift and read the linen as Christ's righteousness credited to believers. Others emphasize the plural and read it as the actual deeds of the saints. The study holds both together: the deeds are real, and they are produced by the Holy Spirit in those who belong to Christ, so the clothing is both the saints' and God's gift (compare Ephesians 2:8–10, where salvation is not by works, yet believers are created for good works that God prepared beforehand).

## The Parable of the Ten Virgins

In Matthew 25:1–13, the "lamps" may well have been torches (sticks wrapped with rags soaked in oil), which burned for only a short time and needed fresh oil to keep going. A torch without a supply of oil would fail during a long wait at night, which is exactly what happens to the five foolish virgins.

The oil has been interpreted in several ways: as the Holy Spirit, as good works, as faith, or as real readiness for the Lord's return. The parable does not specify. Its point is clear from its ending: readiness cannot be borrowed from someone else at the last minute, and the door, once shut, stays shut. The bridegroom's answer, *"Truly, I say to you, I do not know you"* (Matthew 25:12), echoes Matthew 7:23 almost exactly.

## "I Never Knew You": The Biblical Sense of Knowing

The Hebrew verb *yada*, "to know," covers far more than knowing facts. It describes personal, experiential, covenantal knowledge, and it is the word used for sexual union: *"Now Adam knew Eve his wife, and she conceived"* (Genesis 4:1). The same verb describes God's choice of Israel: *"You only have I known of all the families of the earth"* (Amos 3:2), and his knowledge of Jeremiah before he was formed in the womb (Jeremiah 1:5). In the New Testament, Paul writes that *"if anyone loves God, he is known by God"* (1 Corinthians 8:3), and that *"the Lord knows those who are his"* (2 Timothy 2:19). Jesus' *"I never knew you"* (Matthew 7:23) is a denial of that relationship, not a statement that he lacks information about the person.

## The Rider on the White Horse

### Two White Horses

The first seal and Revelation 19 both open with a rider on a white horse, and the contrast is in the crowns. The first rider is given a *stephanos*, a victor's wreath (Revelation 6:2). The rider of Revelation 19 wears many *diadēmata*, royal crowns (Revelation 19:12). The first carries a bow; the second wields a sword from his mouth. The first is given authority; the second is called Faithful and True and judges in righteousness. In the Roman world, a general celebrating a triumph rode into Rome in a chariot drawn by white horses, and a white horse was a recognized symbol of victory; John's first readers would have read both riders as conquerors before reading anything else.

### The Names and Titles

Nearly every element of the description reaches back into earlier Scripture or earlier Revelation. *"Faithful and True"* repeats Christ's self-description to Laodicea, *"the faithful and true witness"* (Revelation 3:14). The eyes *"like a flame of fire"* come from John's first vision (Revelation 1:14) and Daniel 10:6. The sharp sword from his mouth appears in Revelation 1:16 and 2:16, and it draws on Isaiah 11:4, where the shoot of Jesse *"shall strike the earth with the rod of his mouth, and with the breath of his lips he shall kill the wicked,"* and Isaiah 49:2, *"He made my mouth like a sharp sword."* The rod of iron is from Psalm 2:9, where God's anointed King breaks the nations; Revelation applies the same verse to the male child of Revelation 12:5.

*"The Word of God"* is John's name for Christ in John 1:1 and 1 John 1:1. *"King of kings and Lord of lords"* has roots in Deuteronomy 10:17 (*"the Lord your God is God of gods and Lord of lords"*) and Daniel 2:47, where Nebuchadnezzar calls Daniel's God *"God of gods and Lord of kings."* Paul uses the title of Christ in 1 Timothy 6:15, and it appears first in Revelation at 17:14, where the Lamb conquers the beast and the ten kings *"for he is Lord of lords and King of kings."* The name written on the thigh would be visible on a mounted rider, where the robe falls across the leg or where a sword was belted.

### The Robe Dipped in Blood

The robe dipped in blood comes from Isaiah 63:1–6. A warrior comes from Edom in crimson garments, and when asked why his clothing is red, like one who treads the winepress, he answers: *"I have trodden the winepress alone... their lifeblood spattered on my garments, and stained all my apparel."* In Revelation, the blood is on the robe before the battle in 19:19–21 is described. Some interpreters therefore read it as Christ's own blood from the cross; others, in keeping with Isaiah 63 and Revelation 19:15, read it as the blood of his enemies from the winepress. The study reads it as the follow-on to the winepress of Revelation 14:17–20.

## Two Suppers

Revelation 19 contains two meals. The marriage supper of the Lamb (19:9) is a celebration to which the blessed are invited. The great supper of God (19:17) is a summons to the birds to feed on the flesh of kings, captains, mighty men, and horses and their riders. The second comes almost word for word from Ezekiel 39:17–20, where God calls the birds and beasts to *"my sacrificial feast that I am preparing for you"* on the mountains of Israel after the defeat of Gog. The two suppers stand side by side in the same chapter, in the same way the grain harvest and the grape harvest stand side by side in Revelation 14: one for those who belong to the Lamb and one for those who gathered to make war against him.

## Thrown Alive into the Lake of Fire

The beast and the false prophet are the first to be cast into the lake of fire, and they are thrown in alive (Revelation 19:20). Daniel saw the same end for the fourth beast: *"the beast was killed, and its body destroyed and given over to be burned with fire"* (Daniel 7:11). The phrase "lake of fire that burns with sulfur" combines the fire and sulfur that fell on Sodom (Genesis 19:24) with the burning place of judgment in Isaiah 30:33. One detail is significant for questions about whether the lake of fire destroys those thrown into it: a thousand years later, when the devil is thrown in, the beast and the false prophet are still there, and *"they will be tormented day and night forever and ever"* (Revelation 20:10).
