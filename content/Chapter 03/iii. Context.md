## The Proto-Evangelium: Genesis 3:15 in Jewish and Christian Interpretation

Genesis 3:15 holds a unique position in the history of biblical interpretation. The designation *proto-evangelium*, "first gospel," was applied to this verse by Christian interpreters who recognized that God's words to the serpent in the garden contain the earliest articulation of the redemptive storyline: enmity between two offspring, a wounding of the deliverer, and a decisive blow to the adversary.

The verse in its Hebrew original uses the same verb (*shuph*) twice: once for what the offspring of the woman will do to the serpent's head, and once for what the serpent will do to his heel. Translators have long grappled with how to render this symmetry. The Septuagint (the Greek translation of the Hebrew Scriptures, completed before the time of Jesus) uses a masculine pronoun, *autos*, for "he" even though its word for "offspring," *sperma*, is grammatically neuter, a choice that points toward an individual rather than a collective. Early Jewish interpreters also weighed the identity of the "he": some Aramaic Targums read the verse as a promise of victory over the serpent "in the days of King Messiah," while others applied it to the people of Israel collectively. Christian interpretation, from the New Testament onward, read it as pointing to the Messiah.

The word translated "heel" (*ʿaqev* in Hebrew) deserves additional attention. Beyond its anatomical meaning, it can describe the rear of a body of people, as in Genesis 49:19, where Gad *"will attack them at their heels,"* and Joshua 8:13, where it refers to the force positioned behind the city. Read with that sense in view, a strike to the heel suggests a hit from behind, painful and disabling but not fatal to the one who is struck. The strike the serpent delivers is real damage, suffering and temporary reversal, but it does not end the advance. The serpent's own head, by contrast, is crushed: a decisive, fatal blow. The asymmetry in the verse is deliberate.

Paul's letter to the Romans echoes this verse directly: *"The God of peace will soon crush Satan under your feet"* (Romans 16:20). The language is unmistakably drawn from Genesis 3:15, and Paul applies it to the community of believers, suggesting that the crushing of the serpent's head is accomplished through Christ's victory and extended through his people.

## Nachash: What Was the Creature in the Garden?

The Hebrew word *nachash* (translated "serpent" in Genesis 3) is one of the more interesting words in the creation narrative. The noun ordinarily means "snake," but the same three consonants also form the verb "to practice divination" (Genesis 44:5) and sit close to *nechoshet*, "bronze," the metal of the bronze serpent Moses lifted up (Numbers 21:9). Some scholars, Michael Heiser among them, have argued that the word plays on all three ideas: a serpentine, luminous being who deals in hidden knowledge. That reading is debated, but it reminds modern readers that the creature in the garden is not necessarily what the English word conjures.

Several features of the text complicate a straightforward reading of the serpent as simply a snake. First, it speaks, and the text presents this without comment, as though it were not surprising within the world of the garden. Second, the punishment it receives is that it will crawl on its belly and eat dust. A punishment of this kind implies a change from a previous state, something that did not always crawl on its belly. Third, Revelation 12:9 identifies "the ancient serpent" explicitly as the devil and Satan, who "leads the whole world astray." The serpent in Genesis 3 and the dragon in Revelation 12 are the same being.

The picture that emerges from these details is of a magnificent, possibly limbed creature, far closer to what later biblical literature calls a dragon than to a garden snake, through whom Satan operated. The sentence of belly-crawling and dust-eating is a sentence of degradation. What was formidable became a symbol of humiliation, which is why the imagery of the serpent runs through Scripture as a consistent emblem of evil, defeated adversary, and curse.
