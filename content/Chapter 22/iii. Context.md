## Apocalyptic Literature: How Literal Events Get Symbolic Clothing

The interpretive question the session opens with, how much of Revelation is literal and how much is symbolic, is really a question about genre. Revelation identifies itself in its opening verse as an *apokalypsis*, an "unveiling," and it belongs to a recognized body of ancient Jewish literature that includes Daniel, large portions of Ezekiel and Zechariah, and extra-biblical works such as 1 Enoch. Apocalyptic writing has a set of recurring conventions: a human seer is given a vision, often escorted or addressed by an angelic figure; the vision employs symbolic creatures, numbers, and cosmic imagery; and the symbolism is typically resolved either by an explicit interpretation given within the text or by imagery that would have been immediately recognizable to a reader steeped in earlier Scripture.

Daniel 7 is the clearest internal model for how this works. Daniel sees four beasts rise from the sea, imagery as strange as anything in Revelation, and then the vision itself supplies the key: "The four great beasts are four kingdoms that will rise from the earth" (Daniel 7:17). The vision is symbolic. What it depicts is not. This is the pattern apocalyptic literature consistently follows, and it is why most interpreters, whatever their framework, treat Revelation's imagery as pointing to real realities rather than as a self-contained symbol system that means whatever a given reader wants it to mean. Where they differ is on *which* realities, and when: past events in the Roman world, recurring patterns across church history, or events still to come. The genre invites symbolic reading of the *imagery* precisely because it expects real fulfillment of what the imagery describes.

## The Throne Room Vision: Ezekiel 1 and the Cherubim

John's vision of the throne draws directly on Ezekiel's inaugural vision in Ezekiel 1, received about seven centuries earlier, in 593 BC, during the Babylonian exile. Ezekiel describes four living creatures, each with four faces (human, lion, ox, and eagle) and four wings, beneath an expanse above which sits a throne of sapphire, surrounded by brilliant light "like the appearance of a rainbow in the clouds" (Ezekiel 1:28). John's creatures differ in detail: each has a single face, one of the four, and six wings, a feature drawn from the seraphim of Isaiah 6. The correspondence to Revelation 4 is not coincidental; John is seeing, and describing, the same reality the prophets saw, and the shared imagery signals continuity between the visions rather than unrelated events.

Ezekiel's creatures are identified as cherubim (Ezekiel 10:20), a class of angelic being attested from the earliest chapters of the Hebrew Bible. Genesis 3:24 places cherubim with a flaming sword at the entrance to Eden after the expulsion of Adam and Eve, guarding the way to the tree of life. Exodus 25:18–20 has two golden cherubim, their wings overshadowing the mercy seat, forming the throne imagery atop the Ark of the Covenant: the physical furniture of the tabernacle deliberately modeled on a heavenly pattern (Exodus 25:40; Hebrews 8:5). Ezekiel 28:14–16, in an oracle addressed to the king of Tyre but widely read as describing the fall of Satan, identifies the figure being denounced as an "anointed... guardian cherub," present in Eden, until wickedness was found in him. Cherubim, in other words, are not generic angels; they are specifically associated with guarding sacred space and standing in closest proximity to God's own presence, which is exactly the function they perform in both Ezekiel's and John's visions.

## Sealed with Seven Seals: The Legal Document Convention

The description of the scroll in Revelation 5, written on both sides and sealed with seven seals, reflects documentary practice in the ancient world. A scroll written on both sides (an *opisthograph*, in the technical term) was unusual, since scribes normally wrote only on the inner side of a roll, and it suggests a document full to overflowing. The closest biblical parallel is the scroll Ezekiel is handed, "written on both sides," full of "words of lament and mourning and woe" (Ezekiel 2:9–10). Multiple wax seals affixed by witnesses were a recognized feature of formal legal instruments across the Greco-Roman world, particularly wills. The Roman jurist Gaius, writing in the second century, describes wills bearing the seals of seven witnesses (*Institutes* 2.119). A document of this kind could not be opened, and its contents could not take legal effect, until someone with the standing to do so broke the seals.

Read against that background, the scroll in Revelation 5 can be understood as a legal instrument of inheritance: a testament whose contents cannot be executed until it is properly and lawfully opened. On that reading, the angel's question, "Who is worthy to break the seals and open the scroll?" is not a poetic flourish but a real question about legal standing, and the answer, that only the Lion of the tribe of Judah, the Root of David, is qualified, identifies Jesus as the sole rightful heir to whatever the document conveys.

## Lion of the Tribe of Judah, Root of David: Messianic Titles

Both titles applied to Jesus in Revelation 5:5 have deep roots earlier in the Hebrew Bible. "The Lion of the tribe of Judah" draws on Jacob's deathbed blessing over his son Judah in Genesis 49:9–10: "Judah is a lion's cub... The scepter will not depart from Judah, nor the ruler's staff from between his feet, until he to whom it belongs shall come." Judah is identified as the tribe from which kingship, and ultimately the Messiah, would arise, a promise that runs forward through David and into the New Testament's genealogies of Jesus.

"The Root of David" draws on Isaiah 11:1 and 11:10: "A shoot will come up from the stump of Jesse; from his roots a Branch will bear fruit... In that day the Root of Jesse will stand as a banner for the peoples." The image is deliberately paradoxical: a root does not come from a tree, a tree comes from a root. Applied to David's line, the title signals that the Messiah is simultaneously David's descendant and David's source, a claim consistent with the New Testament's insistence that Jesus is both fully human, born into David's line, and fully divine, existing before David and giving rise to him. Revelation 22:16, near the book's close, has Jesus apply this same title to himself directly: "I am the Root and the Offspring of David."
