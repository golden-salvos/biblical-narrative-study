## Michael: "Who Is Like God?"

Michael appears by name five times in the Bible: Daniel 10:13 and 10:21, Daniel 12:1, Jude 9, and Revelation 12:7. In every appearance he is in conflict — contending with the prince of Persia, arising at the start of the great tribulation, disputing with the devil over the body of Moses, and leading the angelic army that expels the dragon from heaven. Jude calls him "the archangel," and Daniel calls him "one of the chief princes" and "the great prince who has charge of your people." Later Jewish tradition, including the Dead Sea Scrolls' *War Scroll*, casts him as the commander of the forces of light in the final battle.

His name is a Hebrew question: *Mi-ka-El* — "Who is like God?" That question has a history in Scripture. After the crossing of the Red Sea, the Song of Moses asks it of the divine council and answers it by implication: *"Who is like you, O Lord, among the gods?"* (Exodus 15:11). No one is. Revelation 13:4 puts the same question into the mouths of the beast's worshipers, turned upside down: *"Who is like the beast, and who can fight against it?"* The chapter is built around that contrast. The angel whose name asks who is like God is the one who defeats the dragon, and the world that follows the beast answers the question wrongly.

## The Woman in the History of Interpretation

Three readings of the woman in Revelation 12 have dominated the church's history.

The reading that identifies her as Israel draws on the imagery of Joseph's dream (Genesis 37:9–11) and on the prophets' repeated picture of Zion as a woman in labor — Isaiah 66:7–8 describes Zion giving birth to a son before her pains come, and Micah 4:10 and 5:3 speak of the daughter of Zion writhing in labor until "she who is in labor has given birth." The woman who produces the Messiah and then flees to be protected in the wilderness is, on this reading, the covenant people as a whole.

The reading that identifies her as Mary developed later and became prominent in medieval Western Christianity, where the image of the woman clothed with the sun and crowned with stars shaped the iconography of Mary as Queen of Heaven. It remains the dominant devotional reading in Roman Catholic tradition, though most Catholic scholars today acknowledge that the primary referent is the people of God, with Mary as the individual through whom that people brought forth the Messiah.

A third reading identifies the woman as the church. This view is common among interpreters who read Revelation symbolically, but it struggles with the chapter's sequence: the church did not give birth to Christ. The Israel reading accounts for the whole chapter — the birth of the Messiah, the flight into the wilderness at the end of the age, and the dragon's war against "the rest of her offspring" — without strain.

One detail in Genesis 37 is worth knowing. When Joseph tells the dream, Jacob asks whether "your mother" will bow down to him, even though Rachel had died in childbirth two chapters earlier (Genesis 35:19). Rabbinic commentators discussed this long ago; common explanations are that Jacob was speaking of the household in general terms, or of Bilhah, Rachel's maidservant, who helped raise Joseph. Either way, the dream's symbolism of the family as a whole — sun, moon, and stars — is what Revelation takes up.

## The Sign in the Sky

Revelation 12 calls the woman and the dragon "signs" (*sēmeion*) that appear "in heaven," and some interpreters take that as a reference to the night sky as well as to the heavenly realm. The best-known modern proposal concerns September 23, 2017, when the sun stood in the constellation Virgo, the moon lay at her feet, the planet Jupiter had spent roughly nine months moving within her in retrograde motion, and the nine stars of the neighboring constellation Leo, joined by the planets Mercury, Venus, and Mars, formed a group of twelve above her head. The brightest star of Leo, Regulus — named "little king" in Latin — belongs to Leo, not Virgo; it is Jupiter, the "king planet" of ancient astronomy, that passes through Virgo in this configuration.

A separate line of study attempts to reconstruct the Star of Bethlehem from astronomical records, often pointing to a series of conjunctions between Jupiter and Regulus in 3–2 BC and to Jupiter's apparent stationary point during retrograde motion as the moment the star "stood over" the place where the child was. These proposals are debated, and none of them is required to read Revelation 12. The chapter's meaning rests on its Old Testament sources, not on any particular alignment of the stars.

## The Seven Heads and Revelation 17

Revelation 13 names the heads but does not explain them. Revelation 17:9–11 does, and the study has already worked through that passage (see [[i. Seventy Sevens|Chapter 16 — Seventy Sevens]]). There John is told the seven heads are seven kings: "five have fallen, one is, the other has not yet come, and when he does come he must remain only a little while." The beast itself "was and is not" and is "an eighth, but it belongs to the seven."

Read from John's vantage point under Roman rule, the five fallen kingdoms are Egypt, Assyria, Babylon, Medo-Persia, and Greece; Rome is the one that "is"; and the seventh is a kingdom still to come after Rome. Chapter 16 identified that seventh kingdom as the historical Islamic caliphate, which rose after Rome's fragmentation, "remained a little while," and was abolished in 1924 — and identified the beast who "was, and is not, and is to come" as the eighth king who belongs to the seven: the caliphate revived.

This session's reading of the mortal wound fits that framework rather than competing with it. The head that "seemed to have a mortal wound" is the seventh — the caliphate, which died in 1924. The healing of the wound is its revival as the Antichrist's kingdom, the eighth king who belongs to the seven. Revelation 13 and Revelation 17 describe the same kingdom from two angles: one pictures a death and a healing, the other a kingdom that "was, and is not, and is to come."

## The End of the Caliphate, 1924

The institution of the caliphate began in 632 with Abu Bakr, the first successor to Muhammad, and passed through the Rashidun, Umayyad, and Abbasid dynasties. After the Mongol sack of Baghdad in 1258 it survived in largely ceremonial form under the Mamluks of Egypt, and the Ottoman sultans took up the title after conquering Egypt in 1517. For roughly four centuries the Ottoman sultan held the office, and the Ottoman Empire at its height governed Turkey, the Balkans, the Levant, Mesopotamia, much of Arabia, Egypt, and the North African coast — the same crescent around Israel described in this session.

World War I ended it. The Ottomans fought on the side of the Central Powers and lost. Before the war was even over, Britain and France had secretly agreed on how to divide the empire's Arab provinces (the Sykes–Picot Agreement of 1916), and the postwar settlement placed Iraq, Palestine, and Transjordan under British mandate and Syria and Lebanon under French mandate (the San Remo conference of 1920). Most of the borders of the modern Middle East date from these arrangements — drawn by European powers on the model of the Westphalian nation-state, a system foreign to the region's own political and religious structure.

In Turkey itself, Mustafa Kemal (later called Atatürk) led the nationalist movement that abolished the Ottoman sultanate in 1922 and established a secular republic in 1923. On March 3, 1924, the Turkish Grand National Assembly abolished the caliphate, and the last caliph, Abdulmejid II, was sent into exile. Attempts to revive the office elsewhere in the following years came to nothing. In the century since, the idea of a restored caliphate has remained a live aspiration in parts of the Islamic world — most visibly, and most violently, in the self-proclaimed caliphate of the Islamic State from 2014 to 2019, which never gained broad recognition.

## Satan in the Heavenly Court

The picture of Satan with standing in heaven is not unique to Revelation. It is anchored in three Old Testament scenes.

In Job 1–2, "the sons of God came to present themselves before the Lord, and Satan also came among them." The Hebrew is *ha-satan* — "the adversary," with the definite article, closer to a title than a personal name. He comes as a prosecutor, questioning Job's motives and receiving limited permission to test him. In Zechariah 3:1–2, the prophet sees Joshua the high priest standing before the angel of the Lord, "and Satan standing at his right hand to accuse him" — the right hand being the position of the accuser in an ancient court. In 1 Kings 22:19–23, the prophet Micaiah sees the Lord enthroned with "all the host of heaven standing beside him," deliberating over how to bring judgment on King Ahab, and a spirit steps forward to volunteer.

Together these texts describe a heavenly court in session, with an adversary who has the right to appear and bring charges. That is the background for Revelation 12:10's title "the accuser of our brothers, who accuses them day and night before our God." The Greek word for accuser, *katēgōr*, is a legal term for a prosecutor. What ends in Revelation 12 is not Satan's existence or his activity on the earth; it is his standing in court. The same legal framework explains why Jesus's sacrifice matters in these terms: the blood of the Lamb answers every charge the accuser could bring.

## Eagle's Wings and Floods: The Old Testament Imagery of Revelation 12:14–16

Both images in the woman's escape come from the story of the Exodus and from the prophets.

The eagle's wings come from Exodus 19:4, spoken at Sinai: "You yourselves have seen what I did to the Egyptians, and how I bore you on eagles' wings and brought you to myself." Deuteronomy 32:11 expands the picture in the Song of Moses: "like an eagle that stirs up its nest, that flutters over its young, spreading out its wings, catching them, bearing them on its pinions." The wilderness in Revelation 12 is a second Exodus wilderness: a place where God carries his people out of reach of their pursuer and feeds them, as he fed Israel with manna. The verb in Revelation 12:6 and 12:14 — the woman is "nourished" there — points the same direction.

The flood imagery draws on a long prophetic tradition of describing invading armies as overflowing water. Isaiah 8:7–8 describes the king of Assyria as the waters of the River rising over its banks and sweeping into Judah "up to the neck." Jeremiah 46:7–8 pictures Egypt's army rising like the Nile. Daniel uses the same language for the end-times conflicts: the end of the city will come "with a flood" (Daniel 9:26), and the king of the North will "overflow and pass through" (Daniel 11:10, 40). The earth opening its mouth to swallow the flood has its own precedent in Numbers 16:32, where the ground opens and swallows Korah's rebellion. Revelation 12 draws on all three: God carries his people as at the Exodus, the enemy pours out an army like a flood, and the ground itself fights on Israel's side.

## Foreknowledge, Predestination, and the Two Systems

The debate over predestination is older than either Calvin or Arminius — Augustine and Pelagius argued over grace and human will in the fifth century — but the two systems named in this session took their classic form in the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries.

John Calvin (1509–1564) taught that God, in his sovereign will, chose before creation those who would be saved, and that this choice is not based on anything he foresaw in them. After Calvin's death, the Dutch theologian Jacobus Arminius (1560–1609) challenged that teaching. His followers, the Remonstrants, set out five points in 1610, and the Reformed churches answered them at the Synod of Dort (1618–1619), whose five heads of doctrine were later summarized by the acronym TULIP: total depravity, unconditional election, limited atonement, irresistible grace, and perseverance of the saints. Arminianism, by contrast, teaches conditional election based on God's foreknowledge of faith, an atonement intended for all, grace that can be resisted, and — in many of its forms — the possibility of falling away.

The debate turns largely on Romans 8:29, Romans 9, and Ephesians 1:4–5. Readers should know how the study's reading of Romans 8:29 sits in that history. The view that God's predestining follows his foreknowledge of who would choose him is, historically, the Arminian reading of the verse, and was Arminius's own. Calvinist interpreters answer that "foreknew" in Scripture often means to know relationally — to set one's love on someone beforehand — rather than to know facts in advance, pointing to texts like Amos 3:2 ("You only have I known of all the families of the earth") and Jeremiah 1:5 ("Before I formed you in the womb I knew you"). The study rejects the Calvinist system outright; its disagreement with Arminianism concerns the system's other conclusions and its tendency, as the study sees it, to rest the weight of salvation on human logic rather than on the text.

## The Mark in Its First-Century Setting

John's word for the mark is *charagma*, a term with concrete meaning in the Roman world. It was used for the official stamp or seal impressed on documents such as bills of sale and contracts, and for the image and inscription struck on coins — including the emperor's name and likeness. Slaves, soldiers, and devotees of certain gods could also be branded or tattooed with the name or symbol of their owner, commander, or deity. A first-century reader would have heard in "the mark of the beast" a sign of ownership and allegiance bound up with commerce: you belong to the one whose name you bear, and you trade in the economy he controls.

The placement on the hand and the forehead also echoes Israel's Scriptures. Deuteronomy 6:8 commands that the words of the Shema be bound "as a sign on your hand" and "as frontlets between your eyes" — the origin of the tefillin worn by observant Jews. Ezekiel 9:4 describes a mark placed on the foreheads of the faithful in Jerusalem before judgment falls, and Revelation 7:3 and 14:1 place the seal and name of God and the Lamb on the foreheads of his servants. The mark of the beast is the counterfeit of these: a public confession of loyalty, worn in the same places, bearing the name of the one who demands worship in place of God.

## Gematria and the Number 666

Neither Hebrew nor Greek had separate numerals in the ancient period; letters of the alphabet doubled as numbers. Any word therefore had a numerical value, the sum of its letters. Jewish interpreters called the practice of reading significance into those values *gematria*; the Greek equivalent was *isopsephy*. It was a familiar device in the ancient world. Graffiti at Pompeii includes the line "I love her whose number is 545."

The most widely held scholarly solution to Revelation 13:18 is Nero Caesar. Written in Hebrew letters as *Nrwn Qsr*, the name totals 666. Written without the final *n* — the Latin spelling *Nero* rather than the Greek *Neron* — it totals 616, and 616 is exactly the variant found in some early manuscripts of Revelation, including a papyrus fragment from Oxyrhynchus (P115). Many scholars read the variant as evidence that early readers themselves understood the number this way. Others note that Nero was dead by the time Revelation was most likely written and read him as a type of a future figure rather than the fulfillment.

The earliest Christian commentator on the number offers counsel very close to this session's approach. Irenaeus of Lyons, writing around 180 AD in *Against Heresies* (5.30), knew of the 616 variant and rejected it, discussed several names that could produce 666, and then advised against settling on any of them. It is safer, he wrote, to wait for the fulfillment of the prophecy than to guess, because if the name were meant to be known in advance it would have been announced plainly.

## Islamic Eschatology and the Mirror-Image Reading

Islamic end-times teaching is drawn primarily from the hadith literature rather than the Qur'an itself, and it varies between Sunni and Shia traditions. Its central figures are the Mahdi, "the rightly guided one," who is expected to rise and establish justice across the Muslim world; *al-Masih ad-Dajjal*, the false messiah and great deceiver; and Isa — Jesus — who returns, defeats the Dajjal, and in many traditions prays behind the Mahdi, breaks the cross, and affirms Islam.

The reading presented in this session is part of a broader interpretive argument, popularized in recent decades by writers such as Joel Richardson (*The Islamic Antichrist*, 2009), that Islamic eschatology is structurally a mirror image of the biblical account. On this reading, the Mahdi corresponds to the biblical Antichrist, and the returning Isa of Islamic tradition — a Jesus who denies his own divinity and the cross — corresponds to the false prophet who points the world toward the beast. Taken together with the dragon, they form the counterfeit trinity of Revelation 16:13.

Two points of accuracy belong beside that argument. Islam itself casts the Dajjal as the end-times deceiver and Jesus as his conqueror, so the tradition regards its own figures as the true ones and the biblical picture as the distortion — which is precisely what a mirror image would look like from the other side. And the mirror-image reading is a minority position within Christian eschatology; most interpreters, historically and today, locate the Antichrist's kingdom elsewhere, most often in a revived form of Rome. The study holds the Islamic reading on the strength of Daniel's geography and the Revelation 13 and 17 imagery, and it is recorded here as the study's position.
