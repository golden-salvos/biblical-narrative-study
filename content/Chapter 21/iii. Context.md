## The Temple of Zeus at Pergamon and "Satan's Throne"

Pergamum (the Latin form of the Greek name Pergamon) sat atop a steep acropolis in the Caicus River valley of northwest Asia Minor and was, from the third century BC, the capital of the independent Attalid kingdom, which was bequeathed to Rome in 133 BC. The city hosted an unusual concentration of pagan religious life: a major sanctuary to Asclepius, the god of healing, drew pilgrims from across the ancient world, and in 29 BC Pergamum became the first city in the province of Asia to receive permission to build a temple to Rome and Augustus, making it a center of emperor worship as well.

The most striking structure, however, was the Great Altar of Zeus: an enormous stepped platform crowned with a colonnaded court, built in the second century BC under King Eumenes II to commemorate Pergamum's military victories, and famous for its monumental frieze depicting the battle of the gods against the giants. The altar's raised base and horseshoe-shaped colonnade give the structure the appearance of a colossal throne overlooking the city, which is why many readers connect it with the place "where Satan has his throne" (Revelation 2:13), though the imperial cult and the Asclepius sanctuary are also strong candidates. The altar was excavated by German archaeologists in the late nineteenth century and now stands reconstructed in the Pergamon Museum in Berlin. The structure that stood as a visual claim to divine authority over the city in John's day is today a museum display rather than a place of worship.

## Balaam, Balak, and the Strategy of Corruption From Within

The full narrative behind the "teaching of Balaam" referenced in both the Pergamum and Thyatira letters spans Numbers 22–25 and 31. Balak, king of Moab, hires Balaam, a diviner from Pethor near the Euphrates (Numbers 22:5) whose reputation for effective blessing and cursing was known well beyond his homeland, to curse the approaching Israelites before they can threaten Moab. Across four separate oracles, Balaam is repeatedly compelled to bless Israel instead of cursing them, delivering some of the most concentrated messianic prophecy found outside the major prophets, including the declaration that "a star will come out of Jacob; a scepter will rise out of Israel" (Numbers 24:17).

Direct cursing having failed completely, Numbers 31:16 reveals that Balaam is later credited with advising the Moabites and Midianites on an indirect strategy: use Moabite and Midianite women to draw Israelite men into both intermarriage and participation in pagan sacrificial meals. Numbers 25 records the result: Israelite men engaging in sexual relations with Moabite women and being drawn into worship of Baal of Peor, provoking a plague that kills 24,000 Israelites before Phinehas's intervention halts it. The strategy succeeded precisely where the direct spiritual assault had failed, which is likely why Revelation returns to Balaam as shorthand for any teaching that corrupts a community of believers from the inside through compromise rather than confronting it from the outside through force.

## Sardis and the Warning of "I Will Come Like a Thief"

Sardis's letter carries a specific historical irony worth noting. The ancient city was renowned for the supposed impregnability of its acropolis, a steep, naturally fortified citadel that nonetheless fell to surprise attack on two occasions centuries apart: to the soldiers of Cyrus the Great in 547 BC, who climbed an unguarded section of the cliff while the defenders assumed it could not be scaled (Herodotus 1.84), and again to Antiochus III in 214 BC, by a similar stratagem. A city famous for having twice been captured by an enemy no one saw coming is the same city Christ warns: "If you do not wake up, I will come like a thief, and you will not know at what time I will come to you." Whether or not this specific history was in view for the letter's original audience, it is a striking match for the image chosen.

## Laodicea: Water, Wealth, and Eye Salve

The letter to Laodicea is full of local color. Laodicea had no adequate water source of its own and piped water in by aqueduct from springs several miles away; neighboring Hierapolis was famous for its hot mineral springs, and nearby Colossae for its cold, fresh water. A popular and widely held explanation of "neither cold nor hot" is that Laodicea's water arrived tepid and mineral-laden, useful neither for healing like hot springs nor for refreshment like cold water. On this reading, "cold" and "hot" are both good; Christ's complaint is that Laodicea is useless, not that it lacks zeal.

The city was also prosperous. It was a banking and commercial center, and when an earthquake struck the region around AD 60, Laodicea rebuilt itself from its own resources without imperial aid (Tacitus, *Annals* 14.27). It was known for a glossy black wool, and the region was associated with a medical school and a famous "Phrygian powder" used for the eyes. Christ's counsel lands on each point: buy gold refined in the fire, white clothes rather than black wool, and salve to put on your eyes, so that you can see.
