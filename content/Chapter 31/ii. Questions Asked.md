*A record of genuine questions raised during this session, with the answers given in the room. This is a companion to the main chapter note, not a replacement for it.*

### Q: Do Jews still celebrate the Feast of Tabernacles today?

Yes. It is one of the seven feasts of the Lord in Leviticus 23, and it is still kept every year. Families build temporary booths outside, eat their meals in them, and some sleep in them, remembering the years Israel spent living in tents in the wilderness while God dwelt among them in the tabernacle. In the Millennial Kingdom the nations will be required to come up to Jerusalem to keep it (Zechariah 14:16–19). In the new heaven and new earth it is no longer kept, because what it pointed to has arrived: God dwelling with his people.

### Q: Is the Sabbath one of the seven feasts?

No. Leviticus 23 opens with the Sabbath, but the Sabbath is weekly. The seven feasts are annual: Passover, Unleavened Bread, First Fruits, Weeks (Pentecost), Trumpets, the Day of Atonement, and Tabernacles.

### Q: Are Passover and Unleavened Bread the same feast?

They are listed together in the same paragraph of Leviticus 23 and kept at the same time, as one festival. But they are distinct feasts and were fulfilled separately. Passover is fulfilled in Jesus as the Passover lamb; Unleavened Bread is fulfilled in him as the sinless sacrifice.

### Q: If the trumpets are judgments, why would the Feast of Trumpets be something to celebrate?

Because of what the last trumpet does. The trumpets and the bowls are a progressive revelation of God's wrath, but at the seventh trumpet the Lord takes control of the earth and begins to reign (Revelation 11:15). The feast is fulfilled at that last trumpet, and what it announces is the King.

### Q: Didn't we talk about "flying the bride"?

An audience member raised the Jewish wedding custom she called "flying the bride," in which the bride is lifted up on a chair while the guests dance, and it fits the picture of Christ and his Bride. The custom harkens back to the way a groom came for his bride. His father would tell him when he had added on to the house enough to have a place for her. The groom would then go through the streets calling his friends, a crowd would gather and follow, and they would fetch the bride, who had to be ready at a moment's notice. She was put in a chair and carried to the wedding feast, which lasted about a week. Jesus said he was going to prepare a place for his people in his Father's house and come back for them (John 14:2–3), and when he comes he catches them up in the air to meet him (1 Thessalonians 4:16–17). The New Jerusalem is that prepared place.

### Q: What is jasper, and what do the colors mean?

The colors are not the point of the description, and their exact meaning is not given. What the text emphasizes is purity and glory: the city is pure gold, *"like clear glass"* (Revelation 21:18), and its street is gold *"like transparent glass"* (21:21). What that looks like is beyond human experience; it is gold so pure it can be seen through.

### Q: Is the New Jerusalem coming down on top of present-day Jerusalem?

No. It comes down onto a new earth, after the first heaven and the first earth have passed away (Revelation 21:1). Everything is new. The New Jerusalem represents everything Jerusalem was, and it completely replaces it.

### Q: Isn't the flesh more than the body — doesn't it include your mind and your will?

Yes. When Paul says sin dwells in the flesh (Romans 7:18), the flesh is not only the physical body. It includes the mind and the will as they operate on their own, apart from the Spirit. That is why the change at salvation is so complete: the flesh, with everything that belongs to it, is separated from the spirit, and the spirit is joined to the Holy Spirit. The flesh still has influence on a believer in this life, and the believer has influence over it, but it no longer has control.

### Q: We sin every day. Won't we still sin in the new creation? How is that any different from us now?

It is different because of where sin lives. Paul locates sin in the flesh (Romans 7:18), not in the spirit. If sin were part of our spirit, it would never die, because the spirit never dies. At conversion the flesh is separated from the spirit and the spirit is joined to the Holy Spirit, which is why a believer now has a choice; but in this life the flesh is still present and still pulls. At death the flesh is gone, and in the resurrection believers are clothed in new bodies with no flesh left in them. In the resurrection body we have been completely separated from the flesh, and sin went with it.

That is the difference between the new creation and the Millennial Kingdom. During the thousand years, when Satan was released, the flesh was still there for him to appeal to, and the nations followed him into rebellion. In the new creation, Satan is never coming back, and there is no flesh left for anything to appeal to. For the first time in human history there is no sin anywhere, and that is what makes it possible for God the Father to dwell with mankind.

### Q: Isn't sin in the heart?

Paul places it in the flesh. The question is whether sin is part of who we essentially are — our spirit — or part of what we will one day leave behind. If it were part of the spirit, it would follow us into eternity. Paul's argument in Romans 6–8 is that sin's foothold is the flesh, which dies. between the new creation and the Millennial Kingdom. ### Q: Doesn't "let the evildoer still do evil" sound fatalistic, as if we can't make a difference?

No. Revelation 22:11 is not a command to stop doing good. The audience member who raised it put the other side well: we are still responsible for our own sins and actions, and still supposed to take a stand and make a difference. That is true, and it does not conflict with the verse. Christians are still to do good, to improve the lives of those around them, and to protect the fatherless, the widows, and anyone who cannot protect themselves. The verse is a picture from God's perspective of how he has built the world: every person makes their own choices, and God lets them. The difference is between influence and control. If God chose not to control humanity's choices, believers have no business trying to dictate how people will behave. Justice for evil comes at the end, and it belongs to God (Romans 12:19).

### Q: Isn't "judge not" about judging whether someone is saved, not about whether an act is right or wrong?

Yes, there is a difference. When Jesus says *"Judge not, that you be not judged"* (Matthew 7:1), he is forbidding his followers to sit in God's seat and decide a person's standing before him. He is not forbidding moral clarity. Saying that robbing a bank is wrong is not the judgment Jesus prohibits; deciding that the robber is beyond God's reach is. Final judgment of persons belongs to God alone, and that is the point behind not trying to dictate how everyone else will behave.

### Q: If Satan and everyone in the lake of fire are finished, what is "outside" the city?

They are finished, but they have not ceased to exist. Satan, the Antichrist, and every human who rebelled are still being tormented forever, and they have to be somewhere. Revelation 22:15 says they are outside the city, because the city is the Bride and nothing unclean enters it. However the geography of the new creation works, the lake of fire is somewhere in it, and it is outside the gates.

### Q: Are the "dogs" outside the city literal dogs?

No. It is an allusion, not a reference to animals. It describes people whose thinking is entirely of the flesh — the unclean, who have no part in the city.

### Q: Will there be animals in the new heaven and new earth? Pets?

Scripture does not say directly. The descriptions of the Millennial Kingdom do include animals — the wolf dwelling with the lamb and the lion eating straw like the ox (Isaiah 11:6–7; 65:25) — but none of the passages about the new heaven and new earth mention them. What is known is the shape of the story. It began in a garden full of animals and plants that God put there. What God is doing at the end is redeeming what Satan corrupted and restoring paradise, so it is entirely within his purview to fill the new earth with animals. There will certainly be trees and rivers. And believers will be different then; whatever is there, it will be perfect, and no one will be disappointed.

### Q: Do we have all the answers about the new creation?

No, and there will still be questions when we get there. It will not matter as much then. What the new creation looks like is far less important than the fact that believers will be in the presence of God and see him face to face. That relationship is the point.

### Q: Aren't you choosing which parts of the Bible to emphasize so that they fit Revelation?

No. The study does not put the parts in. It highlights where Jesus put them, telling the story the way he told it. Following a book means following its author's line of thought: he places things in the story for a purpose and guides the reader through them, and every arc leads to the conclusion — the resolution of the conflict. Everything in the Bible applies to Revelation, not because the study bends it that way, but because Revelation is how he fulfills all things.

### Q: Why isn't the book of Enoch in the Bible?

Because it cannot be proven that Enoch wrote it. That does not mean it should be ignored. Not all of it can be verified as true, but much of it is quoted or reflected in the New Testament: Jude, the brother of Jesus, quotes it directly (Jude 14–15), and Peter refers to the angels who sinned and were held in chains for judgment (2 Peter 2:4). It reflects what was essentially the foundational belief of first-century Jews, so the people in the best position to know took it seriously. Among the Dead Sea Scrolls, copies of 1 Enoch are about as numerous as copies of Isaiah. The community that hid those scrolls, likely the Essenes who populated Qumran, preserved the sacred Scriptures ahead of the coming destruction, and they included Enoch among them. They thought it was worth preserving. *(See the Context note for this session.)*

### Q: Why do the Dead Sea Scrolls matter so much?

Because of their age. Before they were found, the oldest complete Hebrew manuscripts of the Old Testament dated to around AD 1000, about a thousand years removed from Jesus. The scrolls at Qumran are roughly a thousand years older than that. They let us see the Scriptures, and the other writings the community valued, as they were read in the generations just before and during the time of Christ.
