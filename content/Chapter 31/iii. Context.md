## The Feast of Tabernacles

The Feast of Booths (in Hebrew *Sukkot*, from *sukkah*, a booth or hut) is the seventh and final feast of Leviticus 23 (verses 33–43). It began on the fifteenth day of the seventh month, five days after the Day of Atonement, and lasted seven days, with a solemn assembly on the eighth. Israel was commanded to live in booths for the week, *"that your generations may know that I made the people of Israel dwell in booths when I brought them out of the land of Egypt"* (Leviticus 23:43). It was also a harvest festival, the Feast of Ingathering (Exodus 23:16), celebrated once the year's crops were in, and it was the most joyful feast of the calendar: *"you shall rejoice before the Lord your God seven days"* (Leviticus 23:40).

By the first century the feast had gathered two famous ceremonies at the temple. Each morning a priest drew water from the Pool of Siloam and poured it out at the altar, and at night great lamps were lit in the Court of the Women, bright enough, the Mishnah says, to light every courtyard in Jerusalem (*Sukkah* 4–5). John's Gospel sets two of Jesus' most direct claims against that backdrop. *"On the last day of the feast, the great day,"* Jesus stood and cried out, *"If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink"* (John 7:37). Shortly after, still in the temple, he said, *"I am the light of the world"* (John 8:12). Revelation 21–22 closes with the same two images: a city lit by the glory of God, whose lamp is the Lamb, and the river of the water of life flowing from the throne.

### Tabernacling Language

Revelation 21:3 is built on a promise repeated through the Old Testament. In Leviticus 26:11–12, the chapter of blessings, God says, *"I will make my dwelling among you... And I will walk among you and will be your God, and you shall be my people."* Ezekiel 37:27 repeats it for the restored nation: *"My dwelling place shall be with them, and I will be their God, and they shall be my people."* Revelation 21:3 joins the two clauses almost word for word.

The Greek carries the theme. The word translated "dwelling place" is *skēnē*, "tent", the same word the Greek Old Testament uses for the tabernacle, and "he will dwell" is its verb, *skēnoō*, "to pitch a tent." John uses the same verb at the start of his Gospel: *"the Word became flesh and dwelt among us"* (John 1:14), literally "tabernacled among us." The Gospel opens with the Son pitching his tent among mankind; Revelation closes with God himself doing so.

### The Fall Feasts and Their Fulfillment

The four spring feasts are widely read by Christian interpreters as fulfilled in Christ's first coming: Paul calls Christ "our Passover lamb" (1 Corinthians 5:7) and "the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep" (1 Corinthians 15:20), and the Spirit was given on the day of Pentecost (Acts 2:1). Pentecost fell fifty days after First Fruits (Leviticus 23:15–16): roughly ten days after the ascension, which Acts 1:3 places forty days after the resurrection.

There is far less agreement on how the three fall feasts will be fulfilled. A common arrangement links Trumpets to the return of Christ, the Day of Atonement to Israel's national repentance when *"they look on me, on him whom they have pierced"* (Zechariah 12:10), and Tabernacles to the Millennial Kingdom, on the strength of Zechariah 14. The study's arrangement differs in the last two. It places the Day of Atonement at the Great White Throne Judgment, where atonement is finally applied to every person found in the book of life, and it places the fulfillment of Tabernacles in the new creation. Zechariah 14 still fits that reading: during the thousand years the nations keep the feast, as an observance, because what it points to, the Father dwelling with mankind, has not yet happened.

## "The Sea Was No More"

The absence of the sea in Revelation 21:1 has been read in several ways, and they are not exclusive. The most literal reading is a physical change in the new creation. A second takes the sea as the barrier that separated John from the churches; he was exiled on an island (Revelation 1:9). The reading the study takes is the most deeply rooted in Scripture: the sea as the realm of chaos. The unformed deep of Genesis 1:2, the sea monsters Leviathan and Rahab (Psalm 74:13–14; Isaiah 27:1; 51:9–10), the four beasts rising from the sea in Daniel 7, and the beast rising from the sea in Revelation 13:1 all belong to the same picture. Isaiah 57:20 compares the wicked to *"the tossing sea; for it cannot be quiet."* Revelation 21 is the end of that whole line of imagery.

## The New Jerusalem

### Its Dimensions

A stadion was about 607 feet, so 12,000 stadia is roughly 1,380 miles; the ESV footnote gives that figure. A cubit was about 18 inches, making the wall's 144 cubits about 216 feet. The numbers are multiples of twelve (twelve thousand stadia; a wall of twelve times twelve cubits) the same number carried by the twelve gates, twelve tribes, twelve angels, twelve foundations, twelve apostles, twelve jewels, and twelve pearls.

The most important detail may be the shape, which an audience member connected to the Holy of Holies during the session. The only perfect cube in the Old Testament is the Most Holy Place of Solomon's temple: *"twenty cubits long, twenty cubits wide, and twenty cubits high"* (1 Kings 6:20). Revelation 21:22 says there is no temple in the city because God and the Lamb are its temple, and the shape says the same thing from the other side. The entire city is the Holy of Holies. The room that only the high priest could enter, once a year, with blood, has become the home of all God's people.

### River to River

God's promise to Abraham in Genesis 15:18 set the land's limits *"from the river of Egypt to the great river, the river Euphrates."* Whether "the river of Egypt" is the Nile or a smaller watercourse on Egypt's border is debated. The Hebrew here is *nahar*, a river, which most naturally means the Nile; elsewhere the border is given as the *nahal* of Egypt, a seasonal stream usually identified with the Wadi el-Arish in the northern Sinai (Numbers 34:5). The widest borders Israel ever held came under David and Solomon, whose rule reached the Euphrates (1 Kings 4:21), but even then Israel did not occupy the whole territory as its own land. Ezekiel 47:13–48:29 describes the land allotted to the twelve tribes in the age to come.

### Gates Named for the Tribes

The twelve gates bearing the names of Israel's tribes come from the closing verses of Ezekiel, which describe the city of the restored land with three gates on each side, each named for a tribe (Ezekiel 48:30–34). Ezekiel's last sentence gives the city its name: *"And the name of the city from that time on shall be, The Lord Is There"* (Ezekiel 48:35), in Hebrew, *YHWH Shammah*. It is the last word of Ezekiel and the theme of Revelation 21.

### Jewels and Pearls

Isaiah 54:11–12 promises Jerusalem foundations of sapphires, pinnacles of agate, gates of carbuncles, and a wall of precious stones. The twelve stones of Revelation 21:19–20 also overlap with the twelve stones of the high priest's breastpiece, each engraved with the name of a tribe (Exodus 28:17–21), though the two lists are not identical, and some of the ancient names cannot be matched with certainty to modern gemstones. Pearls were among the most prized luxuries of the Roman world, worth more than most gems; Jesus' parable of the merchant who sold everything for one pearl of great value (Matthew 13:45–46) assumes it. Gates each made from a single pearl would have struck John's first readers as extravagance beyond imagining.

## The Wedding Imagery

The custom described in the session as "flying the bride", lifting the bride and groom on chairs, is a feature of Jewish weddings today, but it developed relatively late, in Eastern European Jewish tradition. The picture of a first-century groom preparing a room in his father's house, leaving when his father judged it ready, and coming with a procession for a bride who had to be ready at any hour is a popular reconstruction, drawn loosely from later rabbinic sources and from the Gospels' own wedding parables. The details are not documented as a fixed first-century custom. The parts that Scripture itself supplies are clear enough: a betrothal followed by a period of separation (Matthew 1:18), a night-time procession to meet the bridegroom (Matthew 25:1–13), a wedding feast that could run seven days (Judges 14:12), and Jesus' promise to go, prepare a place in his Father's house, and come back for his own (John 14:2–3).

## No Temple

Ezekiel's vision of a temple (chapters 40–48) is one of the most detailed passages in the prophets, giving its measurements, its courts, its priesthood, its offerings, and the return of the glory of the Lord through the east gate (Ezekiel 43:1–5). Interpreters disagree about how to read it, as a literal temple for the Millennial Kingdom, as a symbolic picture of restored worship, or as an idealized vision of the post-exilic temple. The study reads it literally, as the temple of the thousand years. On that reading, the contrast with Revelation 21:22 is deliberate. The temple and its sacrifices belong to an age in which God still dwells behind a boundary. The New Jerusalem has no boundary, because there is no longer any sin for it to guard against.

## The Flesh and the Spirit in Paul

The Greek word Paul uses is *sarx*, "flesh." It can mean simply the physical body or human nature in its weakness, but in Paul's arguments it most often means human existence as it operates apart from God and under the power of sin. In Romans 7 Paul describes the experience of a person who wants to do good and cannot: *"Now if I do what I do not want, it is no longer I who do it, but sin that dwells within me"* (Romans 7:20). In Romans 6:3–6 he says believers were baptized into Christ's death, *"buried therefore with him by baptism into death,"* so that the old self was crucified and they are no longer enslaved to sin. Galatians 5:16–17 sets the flesh and the Spirit against each other in the life of a believer, which is the ongoing contest the study describes: the flesh still has influence, but it no longer rules.

How Christians describe the "sin nature" varies. The Reformation tradition generally teaches that sin has corrupted the whole person (mind, will, and affections as well as body) and that believers retain a remnant of that corruption until glorification. Others emphasize, as the study does, that Paul locates sin's foothold in the flesh, and that the believer's spirit is made new and joined to the Holy Spirit at conversion. Both views agree on the outcome: in the resurrection, sin no longer has any foothold at all (1 Corinthians 15:42–44, 53).

## The River, the Tree, and Ezekiel 47

Revelation 22:1–2 draws directly on Ezekiel 47:1–12. Ezekiel sees water flowing from under the threshold of the temple, rising until it becomes a river no one can cross, turning the Dead Sea fresh. On both banks grow trees whose leaves do not wither: *"They will bear fresh fruit every month, because the water for them flows from the sanctuary. Their fruit will be for food, and their leaves for healing"* (Ezekiel 47:12). In Ezekiel the river flows from the temple. In Revelation there is no temple, and the river flows from the throne of God and of the Lamb.

The tree of life was last seen in Genesis 3:22–24, when Adam and Eve were driven from the garden and cherubim with a flaming sword were set to guard the way to it. The promise to the overcomer in Ephesus was the right to eat of it (Revelation 2:7). In Revelation 22 the way is open again, and the statement *"no longer will there be anything accursed"* (22:3) answers the curse pronounced in Genesis 3:17.

## Sealed and Unsealed

Daniel was told twice to seal his words. *"But you, Daniel, shut up the words and seal the book, until the time of the end"* (Daniel 12:4), and again, *"Go your way, Daniel, for the words are shut up and sealed until the time of the end"* (12:9). Revelation 22:10 reverses the instruction because the time is near. The next verse also echoes Daniel: *"Many shall purify themselves and make themselves white and be refined, but the wicked shall act wickedly"* (Daniel 12:10) stands behind *"Let the evildoer still do evil... and the holy still be holy"* (Revelation 22:11). Both describe the same reality: at the end, people's choices are revealed and confirmed.

## "Outside Are the Dogs"

"Dogs" was a term of contempt in the ancient Near East, not a term of affection; dogs were scavengers, not household pets. In the Law the word appears as a label for a male cult prostitute (Deuteronomy 23:18). Jewish usage sometimes applied it to Gentiles as unclean (compare Matthew 15:26), and Paul turned the insult around against those who insisted on circumcision, calling them dogs and "those who mutilate the flesh" (Philippians 3:2). Jesus said not to give what is holy to dogs (Matthew 7:6). In Revelation 22:15 the word heads a list of those excluded from the city and stands for everything unclean that cannot enter it (compare 21:27).

## The Serenity Prayer

The prayer referred to in the session (asking God for the serenity to accept what cannot be changed, the courage to change what can, and the wisdom to know the difference) is generally attributed to the American theologian Reinhold Niebuhr, who used it in sermons in the 1930s and 1940s. It became widely known through its adoption by Alcoholics Anonymous.

## 1 Enoch at Qumran

1 Enoch was never part of the Jewish canon or the canon of most of the church, but it was widely read. Jude 14–15 quotes 1 Enoch 1:9 directly, attributing it to *"Enoch, the seventh from Adam,"* and 2 Peter 2:4 and Jude 6 describe angels who sinned and are kept in chains of darkness until judgment, the central story of 1 Enoch 6–16. Several early church writers, Tertullian among them, regarded it as Scripture, and it remains in the canon of the Ethiopian and Eritrean Orthodox Tewahedo churches, the only churches that keep it.

The Dead Sea Scrolls contain fragments of roughly a dozen to two dozen manuscripts of Enochic writings, depending on whether related works such as the Astronomical Book and the Book of Giants are counted separately. For comparison, Isaiah is attested in about twenty-one or twenty-two manuscripts, behind only Psalms and Deuteronomy among biblical books. Some tallies place 1 Enoch above Isaiah; more conservative counts place it somewhat below. Either way, Enoch was among the most copied texts in the collection, which shows how highly the community valued it.

Before the scrolls were discovered (1947–1956), the oldest complete Hebrew manuscripts of the Old Testament were medieval codices: the Aleppo Codex (c. AD 930) and the Leningrad Codex (AD 1008). The Great Isaiah Scroll from Qumran Cave 1 is about a thousand years older. Most scholars identify the Qumran community with the Essenes, a Jewish sect described by Josephus, Philo, and Pliny the Elder, though that identification has its critics. The suggestion that John the Baptist had some connection to the community is a long-standing scholarly speculation, based on his ministry in the same wilderness near the Jordan and on overlaps in practice and language. Nothing in the Gospels states it, and it cannot be confirmed.
