## The Thousand Years in the Early Church

Revelation 20 is the only passage in Scripture that gives the messianic kingdom a length. The phrase "a thousand years" appears six times in verses 2 through 7. Its interpretation divides Christians into three broad camps, named by how each relates the return of Christ to the millennium (from the Latin *mille*, "thousand").

**Premillennialism** holds that Christ returns *before* the thousand years and reigns on the earth during them. This is the position the study takes. It is also the earliest attested Christian reading. Papias of Hierapolis, an early second-century bishop in the same region as the seven churches, taught a literal earthly kingdom after the resurrection. Justin Martyr wrote around AD 155 that he and other orthodox Christians expected a resurrection of the flesh and a thousand years in a rebuilt Jerusalem, citing Isaiah 65 and Revelation 20 by name (*Dialogue with Trypho* 80–81). Irenaeus of Lyons, writing around AD 180, built a full account of the kingdom from Isaiah 11, Isaiah 65, and Ezekiel 37 (*Against Heresies* 5.32–36). The early form of this belief is often called *chiliasm*, from the Greek *chilioi*, "thousand."

**Amillennialism** reads the thousand years symbolically, as the present age between Christ's first and second comings, with the binding of Satan understood as his restraint by the gospel and the first resurrection understood as spiritual rebirth or the reign of the souls of believers in heaven. Augustine had once held a chiliast view and gave it up; his *City of God* (Book 20) made the symbolic reading dominant in the Western church for more than a thousand years, and it remains the position of most Catholic, Orthodox, Lutheran, and Reformed traditions.

**Postmillennialism** holds that Christ returns *after* a long era of gospel success in which the world is progressively Christianized. It flourished among Puritans and in the eighteenth- and nineteenth-century revival movements.

The study's reading rests on the sequence of Revelation itself: the beast and the false prophet are thrown into the lake of fire in 19:20, Satan is bound in 20:2, and the saints who refused the mark come to life and reign in 20:4. Those events follow the return of Christ in Revelation 19, and the prophets' detailed descriptions of a restored Israel under its King — none of which has yet taken place — supply the content of the thousand years.

## The Cosmic Week

The idea that history runs for six thousand years followed by a thousand-year Sabbath is not stated in Scripture, but it is ancient and widespread in both Jewish and Christian sources. It rests on two texts read together: the six days of creation followed by a day of rest (Genesis 2:2–3), and the statement that *"a thousand years in your sight are but as yesterday when it is past"* (Psalm 90:4), which 2 Peter 3:8 restates: *"with the Lord one day is as a thousand years, and a thousand years as one day."*

The Babylonian Talmud records two versions. One teaching divides history into three periods of two thousand years — "two thousand years of desolation, two thousand years of Torah, and two thousand years of the days of the Messiah." Another, attributed to Rav Kattina, says the world will exist for six thousand years and lie fallow for one thousand: as every seventh year is a year of release for the land, so one thousand years in seven will be a Sabbath for the world (*Sanhedrin* 97a). On the Christian side, the *Epistle of Barnabas*, from around AD 100–130, reads Genesis 2 the same way: *"He ended in six days. He meaneth this, that in six thousand years the Lord shall bring all things to an end; for the day with Him signifieth a thousand years"* (*Barnabas* 15:4, Lightfoot translation). Irenaeus repeats it: *"For in as many days as this world was made, in so many thousand years shall it be concluded"* (*Against Heresies* 5.28.3).

The New Testament connects the Promised Land to the Sabbath in the same way the study does. Hebrews 3–4 takes Psalm 95 — *"they shall not enter my rest"* — and reads Israel's entry into Canaan as a preview of a greater rest still to come: *"So then, there remains a Sabbath rest for the people of God"* (Hebrews 4:9).

The arithmetic depends on which chronology is used. The traditional Jewish calendar counts years from creation (*anno mundi*); the year that began in September 2026 is 5787, which would put the start of the year 6000 in AD 2239. Archbishop James Ussher's seventeenth-century chronology, long printed in the margins of English Bibles, dated creation to 4004 BC, which would have completed six thousand years in the late 1990s. The figures differ because the genealogies and reign lengths in Scripture can be totaled in more than one way, which is why the study speaks of roughly six thousand years and treats the Sabbath reading as a pattern rather than a calendar.

## Isaiah 11 and the Second Exodus

Isaiah 11:11 says the Lord will recover his people "a second time." Verse 16 identifies the first: the exodus, *"as there was for Israel when they came up from the land of Egypt."* The regathering at the end of the age is a second exodus.

The places named in verse 11 cover the entire known world of Isaiah's day. **Pathros** is Upper (southern) Egypt; **Cush** is the land south of Egypt along the Nile, in present-day Sudan; **Elam** is southwestern Iran; **Shinar** is Babylonia, in southern Iraq; **Hamath** is a city on the Orontes River in Syria, present-day Hama; and **the coastlands of the sea** are the islands and shores of the Mediterranean. The scattering runs to every point of the compass, and so does the return.

**Assyria**, the first name on the list and the source of the highway in verse 16, was the empire of northern Mesopotamia. Its heartland lay along the upper Tigris, in what is now northern Iraq, around the cities of Assur and Nineveh (near present-day Mosul), and at its height the empire extended across northern Syria and into southeastern Anatolia. Assyria carried the northern kingdom of Israel into exile after the fall of Samaria in 722 BC (2 Kings 17:6), which is why Isaiah lists it first among the places from which Israel must be recovered.

## Ephraim, Judah, and the Two Sticks

The kingdom divided around 930 BC, after the death of Solomon, when the ten northern tribes rejected his son Rehoboam and made Jeroboam king (1 Kings 12). The northern kingdom took the name Israel, and the prophets often call it **Ephraim** after its largest tribe, or **Joseph**, after Ephraim's father. The southern kingdom, **Judah**, kept Jerusalem and the line of David. The north fell to Assyria in 722 BC; the south fell to Babylon in 586 BC.

Ezekiel 37:15–28 addresses that division directly. The two sticks — one for Judah, one for Joseph — are joined into one in the prophet's hand, and the Lord promises one nation, one king, and one shepherd. Isaiah 11:13 makes the same promise in terms of the two kingdoms' old hostility: *"Ephraim shall not be jealous of Judah, and Judah shall not harass Ephraim."* No reunification of that kind has occurred in the twenty-nine centuries since the split.

## The Valley of Dry Bones: Two Readings

Most modern interpreters read Ezekiel 37:1–14 as a vision of national restoration. The basis is verse 11, where the exiles say, *"Our bones are dried up, and our hope is lost; we are indeed cut off"* — an idiom of despair. On that reading, the Lord answers their saying in its own terms: a nation that considers itself dead and buried will be brought back to life and planted in its land.

Others, including this study, read the passage as an actual raising of the dead. The argument rests on the Lord's own interpretation, which does not leave the language of graves behind: *"I will open your graves and raise you from your graves, O my people. And I will bring you into the land of Israel"* (37:12–13).

The debate is old. The Talmud records rabbis arguing over the same question (*Sanhedrin* 92b). Rabbi Eliezer held that the dead Ezekiel revived stood up, sang praise, and died again. Rabbi Eliezer the son of Rabbi Yose the Galilean held that they went up to the land of Israel, married, and had sons and daughters — and Rabbi Judah ben Bathyra claimed to be one of their descendants. Rabbi Judah gave the famously ambiguous answer *"it was truth; it was a parable."* The second view in that discussion is close to the study's: a real raising to ordinary mortal life, followed by settlement in the land.

The vision held a central place in Jewish hope for resurrection. The synagogue at Dura-Europos, on the Euphrates in eastern Syria, painted around AD 244, devotes one of its largest wall panels to Ezekiel 37: the hand of God, the scattered limbs, and the revived dead standing together.

The same hope shapes the Jewish cemetery on the Mount of Olives, in continuous use for about three thousand years. Graves there face the Temple Mount across the Kidron Valley, and Jewish tradition holds that the resurrection of the dead will begin on that mountain when the Messiah comes, drawing in part on Zechariah 14:4, where the Lord's feet stand on the Mount of Olives.

## A New Heart and the Outpoured Spirit

The promise of Ezekiel 36:26–27 — a new heart, a new spirit, a heart of flesh in place of a heart of stone, and God's own Spirit within — appears earlier in Ezekiel in almost the same words (Ezekiel 11:19–20). It belongs with Jeremiah's promise of a new covenant written on the heart (Jeremiah 31:31–34), and the New Testament takes up both: Paul contrasts the letter written *"not on tablets of stone but on tablets of human hearts"* (2 Corinthians 3:3), and Hebrews 8 quotes Jeremiah in full as fulfilled in Christ.

Ezekiel holds God's gift and human responsibility together. The same prophet who records God saying *"I will give you a new heart"* also records him commanding, *"Cast away from you all the transgressions that you have committed, and make yourselves a new heart and a new spirit! Why will you die, O house of Israel?"* (Ezekiel 18:31). The tension raised in this session's discussion is built into the book itself.

In the Old Testament, the Spirit coming upon a person did not guarantee lasting faithfulness. The Spirit rushed upon Samson repeatedly (Judges 14:6, 19; 15:14), and later *"the Lord had left him"* (Judges 16:20). The Spirit came upon Saul so that he prophesied (1 Samuel 10:10), and later *"the Spirit of the Lord departed from Saul"* (1 Samuel 16:14). David, after Bathsheba, prayed, *"Take not your Holy Spirit from me"* (Psalm 51:11). Ezekiel 39:29 uses the verb "pour out" for the Spirit given to the house of Israel, the same language Joel 2:28 uses for the Spirit poured out *"on all flesh,"* which Peter cites at Pentecost (Acts 2:17).

## Daniel 7:12: Dominion Removed, Lives Prolonged

Daniel 7:12 is unusual. The fourth beast is killed and burned, but the earlier beasts lose their dominion while their lives are *"prolonged for a season and a time."* Historically, that is how the first three kingdoms ended. Babylon was absorbed by Persia, Persia by Alexander, and Alexander's empire by its successors, but the peoples, cities, languages, and cultures of each continued under the next power. The Greek language and culture that Alexander spread outlived his empire by centuries. The verse describes nations stripped of sovereignty but not annihilated, and the study applies the pattern to the nations that survive the Antichrist's destruction and live on in the kingdom without independent authority.

## Zechariah 14 and the Feast of Tabernacles

### The Feast

The Feast of Booths (Hebrew *Sukkot*), or Tabernacles, is the last of the seven appointed times of Leviticus 23, held for seven days beginning on the fifteenth day of the seventh month, in the autumn (Leviticus 23:33–43). It had two meanings. It was the harvest festival of ingathering at the end of the agricultural year (Exodus 23:16; Deuteronomy 16:13–15), and it commemorated the wilderness, when Israel lived in temporary shelters and God dwelt among them in the tabernacle (Leviticus 23:42–43). It was one of the three pilgrimage feasts at which every Israelite male was to appear before the Lord in Jerusalem (Deuteronomy 16:16). Zechariah 14 extends that obligation to the nations.

### Why the Punishment Is Rain

The penalty in Zechariah 14:17 fits the feast. Sukkot falls at the turning of the seasons, just before the autumn rains on which the next year's harvest depends. The Mishnah says that the world is judged at four times each year, and that at the Feast of Tabernacles it is judged "for water" (*Rosh Hashanah* 1:2). The formal prayers for rain in the synagogue liturgy begin at the end of the feast. In the Second Temple period, each day of the festival included a water-libation ceremony, in which water drawn from the Pool of Siloam was poured out at the altar amid celebration (Mishnah *Sukkah* 4:9–5:4). It was on *"the last day of the feast, the great day,"* that Jesus stood and cried out, *"If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink"* (John 7:37–38). Zechariah 14:8 has living waters flowing out of Jerusalem itself.

Egypt receives separate mention because it has almost no rain; its water comes from the Nile. Withholding rain would not touch Egypt, so Zechariah specifies that Egypt receives *"the plague with which the Lord afflicts the nations"* instead (14:18).

### The Nations at Sukkot

The feast already looked toward the nations. Numbers 29:12–34 prescribes bulls offered across the seven days in a descending count — thirteen, twelve, eleven, ten, nine, eight, seven — for a total of seventy. The Talmud explains the seventy bulls as offerings on behalf of the seventy nations of the world (*Sukkah* 55b), the same seventy nations of the Table of Nations in Genesis 10. In Zechariah 14, those nations come up to keep the feast themselves.

### "Holy to the Lord" and the Trader

The words inscribed on the bells of the horses in Zechariah 14:20, *"Holy to the Lord,"* are the inscription that was engraved on the gold plate on the high priest's turban (Exodus 28:36). In the kingdom, the high priest's holiness extends to the horses and the cooking pots.

The last line of the book, *"there shall no longer be a trader in the house of the Lord of hosts"* (14:21), uses the Hebrew *kena'ani*. The word is the ordinary term for a Canaanite, but because the Canaanites, and especially the Phoenician coastal cities, were the great merchants of the ancient Near East, it also came to mean "trader" (Proverbs 31:24; Hosea 12:7; Zephaniah 1:11; compare Isaiah 23:8). Translations divide over which sense is meant here, and both make sense in context: the temple will no longer be a place of commerce, and no one unclean or outside the covenant will defile it. The first reading lies in the background of Jesus clearing the merchants from the temple courts (John 2:13–16).

## Gog and Magog After the Millennium

In Ezekiel 38–39, Gog is a ruler, *"of the land of Magog, the chief prince of Meshech and Tubal,"* who leads a coalition against a restored Israel and is destroyed on the mountains of Israel. Revelation 20:8 turns the two names into a pair, *"Gog and Magog,"* describing the nations Satan gathers from the four corners of the earth after the thousand years. By John's time the phrase had become shorthand in Jewish tradition for the final assault of the nations on Israel; later rabbinic literature refers to *milhemet Gog u-Magog*, "the war of Gog and Magog," as the great battle of the messianic age. Revelation uses the familiar name for a final rebellion that takes place a thousand years after the battle at Armageddon. As in Ezekiel, the battle is ended by God alone, with fire from heaven (compare Ezekiel 38:22; 39:6).

## The Great White Throne and the Books

The scene of Revelation 20:11–15 draws on Daniel's throne vision: *"the court sat in judgment, and the books were opened"* (Daniel 7:10). Scripture speaks of two kinds of record. There are books of deeds, according to which *"the dead were judged... according to what they had done"* (Revelation 20:12; compare Ecclesiastes 12:14; Romans 2:6). And there is the book of life, the record of those who belong to God (Exodus 32:32–33; Psalm 69:28; Daniel 12:1; Philippians 4:3). Malachi 3:16 adds *"a book of remembrance"* written before the Lord of those who feared him.

The note that *"the sea gave up the dead who were in it"* answers an ancient fear: that those lost at sea, never buried, were beyond reach. No one is beyond reach of this judgment.

## The Saints and Judgment

The New Testament does speak of the saints judging. Paul asks the Corinthians, *"Do you not know that the saints will judge the world?... Do you not know that we are to judge angels?"* (1 Corinthians 6:2–3). Jesus promises the twelve that they will sit on twelve thrones *"judging the twelve tribes of Israel"* (Matthew 19:28), and Revelation 20:4 sees thrones occupied by those *"to whom the authority to judge was committed,"* echoing Daniel 7:22, where *"judgment was given for the saints of the Most High."* In the Hebrew Bible, to "judge" often means to govern — the judges of Israel were its rulers — and interpreters commonly read these passages as describing the saints' share in Christ's reign. The study draws the line at final judgment: the saints rule under Christ, but the verdict of the Great White Throne belongs to him alone (John 5:22, 27).

## Those Who Never Heard

The discussion of fairness in this session drew on texts that the church has always used to address people who never heard the gospel. Romans 1:19–20 says God's eternal power and divine nature have been clearly perceived in creation, so that people are without excuse. Romans 2:14–16 adds conscience: Gentiles who do not have the law show that *"the work of the law is written on their hearts,"* and God will judge *"the secrets of men"* on the day of judgment. Paul's sermon at Athens (Acts 17:22–31) begins from an altar *"to the unknown god"* and argues that God set the times and boundaries of every nation *"that they should seek God, and perhaps feel their way toward him and find him."* Abraham, counted righteous for believing God (Genesis 15:6) centuries before the gospel was preached, is Paul's own model for how faith is credited (Romans 4).

Christian thinkers have drawn different conclusions from these passages, from the view that explicit faith in Christ is necessary in every case to the view that God may save some who respond in faith to the light they were given. The study's position is that God judges the heart, knows every choice before it is made, and is just in every judgment, and it does not claim to know the details of how he applies that justice to each person.
