> *Questions raised during the session, with answers drawn from the teaching.*

---

### Q: If being thrown into the lake of fire kills you, why isn't Satan thrown in at the same time as the Antichrist and the false prophet?

The beast and the false prophet are captured and thrown alive into the lake of fire at the end of Revelation 19 (verse 20). Satan is not. In Revelation 20:1–3 he is bound and sealed in the bottomless pit for a thousand years, and the text says he "must be released for a little while" afterward. His release has a purpose: the people living through the thousand years still have free will, and at the end of that period the choice each of them has made is brought into the open. Only after that is the devil thrown into the lake of fire (Revelation 20:10).

### Q: Is the thousand years a gift to Israel? Is Israel the only one receiving it?

It is a gift to Israel: the time in which God fulfills the covenant promises he made to the patriarchs and their descendants, and in which the nation lives under the Messiah it rejected. But Israel is not the only people on the earth during it. Isaiah 2 has all the nations streaming to Jerusalem to be taught, Daniel 7:12 has the earlier kingdoms' lives "prolonged for a season and a time" after their dominion is removed, and Zechariah 14:16 requires the survivors of the nations to go up to Jerusalem every year. The Gentile nations that survive the end are present in the kingdom.

### Q: Where does the teaching that the Watchers taught mankind war come from?

From 1 Enoch, in the account of the Watchers' descent and judgment (1 Enoch 6–16). 1 Enoch 8:1 names Azazel as the one who taught men to make swords, knives, shields, and breastplates, and showed them the metals of the earth and how to work them. Weaponry and metallurgy were part of the forbidden knowledge for which the Watchers were judged, and Isaiah 2:4 ("neither shall they learn war anymore") describes that instruction ending in the kingdom.

### Q: When Isaiah 11 says "his people," does that mean the Jews?

Yes. The passage is about the Jews. The Lord recovers the remnant of his people from Assyria, Egypt, Cush, Elam, and the coastlands, gathers "the banished of Israel" and "the dispersed of Judah" from the four corners of the earth, and ends the hostility between Ephraim and Judah, the northern and southern kingdoms. It is the regathering and reunification of the nation.

### Q: Does "Assyrian" mean Christian?

No. The Assyrians are a people. Ancient Assyria was the empire of northern Mesopotamia that carried the northern kingdom of Israel into exile, and its land, going back to Nimrod (Genesis 10:11), lies in the region of present-day northern Iraq and southeastern Turkey. Modern Assyrians are the descendants of that population, and most of them today happen to be Christians (which is likely the source of the confusion), but the name identifies an ethnicity, not a religion.

### Q: Does Ezekiel 37 actually say the Jews are raised from the dead? Isn't the valley of dry bones symbolic?

Many interpreters read it as symbolic of national restoration: the exiles say their hope is lost, and the "graves" are the graves of exile. This study reads it more literally. The Lord interprets the vision himself: "Behold, I will open your graves and raise you from your graves, O my people. And I will bring you into the land of Israel" (Ezekiel 37:12). The bones are "the whole house of Israel," not part of it, and in this study's reading the passage describes an actual raising, held with some caution. The raising described is like that of Lazarus: a return to mortal life, not the resurrection into glorified bodies that the church receives at the Lord's return.

### Q: Are the Jews of all of history raised, or only some?

That is what the text appears to say. The phrase is "the whole house of Israel" (Ezekiel 37:11), and Ezekiel 39:28 adds, "I will leave none of them remaining among the nations anymore." Scripture does not describe a parallel raising of the Gentile dead before the thousand years; the rest of the dead are raised at the end of the thousand years, at the Great White Throne (Revelation 20:5, 12–13).

### Q: If Ezekiel 38–39 is the Battle of Armageddon, which comes before the kingdom, why is it written after the kingdom passages of Ezekiel 36–37?

Ezekiel's prophecies are not arranged in strict chronological order. Chapters 36–37 describe the restoration of Israel, and chapters 38–39 describe the battle that comes before it. Ezekiel 39 itself shows the order: after the battle and the birds' feast on the fallen, verses 21–29 return to the restoration, "now I will restore the fortunes of Jacob... when I pour out my Spirit upon the house of Israel", placing it after the battle.

### Q: Is "my servant David" in Ezekiel 37 David himself, or Jesus?

Jesus. Isaiah 11 has already identified the coming King as the shoot from the stump of Jesse, David's father. The Davidic Covenant promised that a descendant of David would sit on the throne of Israel forever (2 Samuel 7:13–16), and Ezekiel 37:25 says "David my servant shall be their prince forever." That is the son of David reigning, not David raised to the throne.

### Q: If God gives Israel a new heart and pours out his Spirit on them, isn't that the same as salvation? Doesn't that take away their choice?

This was the most contested question of the session, and it was not fully resolved in the room.

The answer given is that the pouring out of the Spirit and the presence of the Lord do not remove choice. The Spirit came upon Saul, and Saul still rejected God (1 Samuel 10:10; 15:23; 16:14). David had the Spirit and still sinned with Bathsheba, though he returned to the Lord (Psalm 51:11). Satan, before his rebellion, knew God as he is and had nothing evil in the world to draw him away, and he still rebelled. On this view, pouring out the Spirit is not the same as being born again: the poured-out Spirit is still God's Spirit, offered, while being born again is the joining of a person's spirit with his, which cannot happen without that person's choice. Reading Ezekiel 36 as God overriding Israel's will is an assumption the text does not state.

Several in the room disagreed, reading the promise of a new heart and a heart of flesh in place of a heart of stone (Ezekiel 36:26) as describing what happens when a person receives Christ and is born again (John 3:5–6). An audience member connected it to Pharaoh: God foreknew what Pharaoh would choose, gave him the choice, and hardened him in it, so foreknowledge and choice are not in conflict. One audience member asked whether this meant the teaching rejects "once saved, always saved." The answer was that the question assumes the outpoured Spirit means the people receiving it are saved, which is the assumption under dispute; the passage is not about believers losing salvation. The two sides agreed to disagree, with one point held in common: free choice cannot be removed from any part of the equation.

### Q: In Zechariah 14, if a nation doesn't go up to the Feast of Tabernacles, how long does the drought last?

The text does not say. Because the feast comes every year and the requirement is to go up "year after year" (Zechariah 14:16), the natural reading is that the withheld rain lasts until the next feast.

### Q: Why does Zechariah 14:21 say there will no longer be a "trader" (or a "Canaanite") in the house of the Lord?

The Hebrew word, *kena'ani*, means both "Canaanite" and "merchant," because the Canaanites were known as traders; English translations and footnotes vary between the two. Read as "trader," it recalls Jesus overturning the tables of the money changers and merchants in the temple. Read as "Canaanite," it describes the house of the Lord with no one in it who is unclean or outside the people of God. Either way, the point of the verse is that everything in Jerusalem, down to the cooking pots, is holy to the Lord. (See the Context note for this session.)

### Q: Who sits on the thrones in Revelation 20:4? Are they the twenty-four elders?

The twenty-four elders are in heaven, around the throne. The thrones of Revelation 20:4 belong to "those to whom the authority to judge was committed" during the thousand years on the earth. Daniel 7:22 says the time came "when the saints possessed the kingdom," so those reigning are the saints. Whether each believer has a throne of their own is beside the point; what Scripture says is that the saints will reign and rule with him.

### Q: Does Revelation 20:4 mean only those who were beheaded reign with Christ?

No. The martyrs are named because martyrdom is the defining characteristic of the Great Tribulation; those living in the last days who refuse the mark will be beheaded. Daniel places no qualification on the saints who receive the kingdom, and Revelation 20:6 pronounces the blessing on everyone who "shares in the first resurrection."

### Q: Aren't there really three resurrections: Christ's, the church's, and the one after the millennium?

Scripture counts it differently. Christ's resurrection is called the firstfruits (1 Corinthians 15:20, 23) and stands apart from the sequence. "The first resurrection" in Revelation 20:5–6 is the resurrection of the saints at the Lord's return: the gathering of the church, when believers receive their resurrection bodies. "The rest of the dead" are raised after the thousand years and stand before the Great White Throne.

### Q: Do people in the Millennial Kingdom still have a choice?

Absolutely. That is why Satan is released at the end of the thousand years (Revelation 20:7–8). Everyone on the earth in that period lives under perfect justice, in peace, with God ruling in person. Those who rebel against that, knowing him as he is, have made the same choice Satan made.

### Q: What is Gog and Magog in Revelation 20?

The names come from Ezekiel 38–39, where Gog of the land of Magog, prince of Meshech and Tubal (peoples of Asia Minor), leads the coalition against Israel. In Revelation 20:8 the names stand for the peoples Satan gathers from the four corners of the earth after his release. Why the names reappear a thousand years after the battle Ezekiel describes, Scripture does not explain.

### Q: Is the lake of fire only for the beast, the false prophet, and the devil?

No. Revelation 20:15 says that anyone whose name is not found written in the book of life is thrown into the lake of fire. When a person dies, they are either with God or without him. Death ends the physical body, but it is not the end; the spirit lives on.

### Q: Is the beast the devil?

No. The beast is the Antichrist, a man. The false prophet is his religious spokesman. The devil (Satan, the dragon) is the spiritual power behind them both. Revelation 20:10 says the devil is thrown into the lake of fire "where the beast and the false prophet were."

### Q: If the Jews are brought back to life to receive the kingdom, do Gentiles get a second chance too? Isn't that unfair?

From what the text says, no; Scripture describes no comparable raising of the Gentile dead before the thousand years. That answer was given with a caveat: it is a reading of what is written, not a claim to know every detail of God's plan. What it promises Israel rests on covenants God made with Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and David; apart from the covenant with Noah, made with all humanity, there are no corresponding covenants with the Gentile nations. The Gentiles have had their own season, the time of the Gentiles, and God used Israel as the vessel through which Christ came to the world. No people has suffered more in this life than the Jews.

As for those who never heard the gospel: God has made himself known through creation (Romans 1:19–20; Psalm 19:1), Abraham was counted righteous for believing God before anyone knew the name of Jesus (Genesis 15:6), and the book of life is written from the foundation of the world because God knows what each person will choose. God judges the heart. The book of Job shows how badly human reasoning about God's justice can go wrong: Job's friends sounded logical, and all of them were wrong. Some in the room continued to find this hard, and that struggle was acknowledged as honest. The answer offered was trust: God is just, he sees what we cannot, and we are not placed in the seat of judgment.

### Q: What about people who have near-death experiences, who die, meet Jesus, come back, and become believers?

They were allowed to go through that experience. It is not a pattern Scripture sets out, and it does not change the principle: God gives every person the ability to know him, and the response is each person's own. A saying that is not from Scripture makes the point: the same sun that hardens the clay softens the wax. The sun is the same; the response is different. An audience member objected that the analogy implies people are made clay or wax and so have no choice. The analogy breaks down there, and it is not meant to be pressed further; Scripture does not teach that God makes some people unable to respond.

### Q: Wasn't it Paul who told a city about its altar to an "unknown god"?

Yes, in Athens, at the Areopagus (Acts 17:22–31). Paul told the Athenians that the God they worshiped as unknown was the Creator of the world, who "made from one man every nation of mankind... that they should seek God, and perhaps feel their way toward him and find him" (Acts 17:26–27). It is the same point Romans 1 makes: creation itself testifies to the Creator.

### Q: Won't the saints judge with Christ?

The saints will rule and reign with him, but they will not decide anyone's eternal destiny. No believer sends anyone to hell at the Great White Throne; that judgment belongs to the one seated on the throne. That is part of why Scripture says so much about not judging (Matthew 7:1): God does not want his people in the seat of judgment. (Scripture does speak of the saints judging in some sense: see the Context note for this session.)

### Q: What does it look like to rule and reign with Christ?

The Watchers show what it was supposed to look like: to watch over, protect, and guide. They were judged in part because they interfered in human affairs for themselves. Reigning with Christ is the opposite: leading the way Jesus would in that position. An audience member added that ruling also means giving consequences for bad behavior. It does, but Christ remains the authority over those consequences; the kingdom is not a democracy, and the saints do not decide what happens to other human beings. The one requirement for ruling well is love (John 13:35).

### Q: Is Revelation hard to understand on purpose, because of Satan?

Satan is not broadcasting the plan. The Lord says, "seek, and you will find" (Matthew 7:7). The details take curiosity and effort to put together, but the story itself is simple: a love story told from the beginning to the end.
