### Q: If Jesus is God, why does Revelation 10 call him "a mighty angel"? Doesn't that support the argument some groups make that Jesus is a created being rather than God himself?

The word translated "angel" simply means messenger. It describes a role, not a species. Scripture applies this same language to the pre-incarnate Christ well before Revelation — when Moses asked whether the Lord would personally go with Israel, God answered, "I am sending my angel with you," and it was "the angel of the Lord" who threw the Egyptian army into confusion from the pillar of fire. Being called an angel in a given scene describes what Jesus is doing in that moment: appearing, delivering a message, standing as God's visible representative. It does not describe what he is.

The argument that this kind of language makes Jesus a created being does not survive contact with Hebrews, whose opening chapters exist specifically to rule it out. Jesus is not ranked as the best of the angels. He is placed in a different category entirely — the one they are commanded to worship, the one through whom they themselves were made. He also is not limited to one form of appearing: he came as a burning bush, as a man who wrestled Jacob, as a commander with a drawn sword, and as the glowing, cloud-robed figure of this scene. The form changes because the form was never the point. The one taking the form does not.

### Q: Is the little scroll in Revelation 10 the same as the scroll with seven seals from earlier in Revelation — is it the title deed to the earth?

No. The seven-sealed scroll — the one only the Lamb was found worthy to open — is the title deed to creation itself. The little scroll here is a different object: already open, much smaller, and handed directly to John. It is patterned after the scroll Ezekiel is given to eat in Ezekiel 2–3, and it carries the same kind of content: a prophetic commission, a message John is told to carry to the nations, not a legal claim on creation.

### Q: If this figure is Jesus, and Jesus is God, why does he need to swear an oath by someone else — doesn't swearing imply there's a higher authority than him?

It doesn't, because this is exactly how God swears oaths elsewhere in Scripture, and the reasoning is stated directly in Hebrews: when God made his promise to Abraham, "since there was no one greater for him to swear by, he swore by himself." An oath ordinarily calls on a greater power as a guarantee. Since nothing is greater than God, he provides his own guarantee — his own eternal existence stands behind his own word. That is the same pattern behind God's declaration in the Song of Moses, "as surely as I live forever," and it is the pattern here: the angel swears "by him who lives forever" because that is simply God swearing by himself. The oath does not point to something above him. It points back to him.

### Q: How do the two witnesses' three-and-a-half-year ministry and the seventh trumpet fit together in time — if the church is already in heaven by the time the trumpets are sounding, how are the witnesses still down on earth prophesying afterward?

Revelation does not always move in a straight line. Chapter eleven picks up one thread — the two witnesses — and follows it from its beginning at the Abomination of Desolation all the way through their death and resurrection near the end of the tribulation, before returning to mark the next major point on the main timeline: the seventh trumpet. The witnesses' ministry runs concurrently with the trumpet judgments; the text simply finishes narrating their whole story as a unit before picking the broader sequence back up. It is a change in narrative focus, not a jump forward or backward in the actual events themselves.

### Q: The two witnesses die and are then resurrected — isn't that different from what happened to Elijah, who was taken up into heaven without ever dying at all?

Yes, and that distinction is exactly why the leading candidates for who they are turn out to be Enoch and Elijah rather than someone who already died in the ordinary way. Elijah went up in a whirlwind without tasting death; Enoch, per Genesis, "was no more, because God took him away." They are the only two people in Scripture who have never yet died. The two witnesses of Revelation 11, by contrast, are genuinely killed and then genuinely raised — which fits the pattern of everyone who has already died once needing to die once, per Hebrews, while leaving open the possibility that these two are the ones still owed that death. The details are worked out further in the Context notes for this chapter.
