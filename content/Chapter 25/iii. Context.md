## The Identity of the Two Witnesses

Revelation names neither witness, and the debate over who they are is nearly as old as the book's circulation. Three candidates dominate the discussion, and each rests on a different piece of evidence in the text.

The case for Enoch and Elijah is the oldest recorded position, held by the earliest church fathers who addressed the question directly — Tertullian, Irenaeus, and Hippolytus all identified the witnesses this way. Their reasoning ran from Hebrews 9:27: it is appointed for people to die once. Genesis 5:24 says Enoch "was no more, because God took him away," and 2 Kings 2:11 records Elijah taken up in a whirlwind — neither man is recorded as having died. Since Revelation 11 shows the two witnesses being killed, the Fathers reasoned that the witnesses had to be two people who had not yet died at all, so that their deaths in Jerusalem would be the one death appointed to them, still outstanding.

The case for Moses and Elijah rests on the miracles the text assigns the witnesses: power to turn water into blood and strike the earth with plagues echoes Moses' confrontation with Egypt, while power to shut up the sky so it will not rain echoes Elijah's drought in 1 Kings 17. Both men also appear together with Jesus at the Transfiguration, which some read as a preview of their return. The difficulty with this pairing is Deuteronomy 34:5–7, which records plainly that Moses died and was buried by the Lord in Moab — directly undercutting the "has not yet died" argument that makes the Enoch-and-Elijah case work in the first place.

A third position, more common in later and more allegorical readings of Revelation, treats the two witnesses symbolically rather than as individuals — as the church's corporate testimony, or as the Law and the Prophets, or as two representative bodies of believers rather than two men. This reading became more common after the fourth century, as apocalyptic interpretation generally shifted away from expecting a literal, future fulfillment.

The study's position favors the historical, patristic reading: Enoch and Elijah, on the strength of their shared and singular status as the only two people in Scripture who have not yet experienced death. Whichever reading is correct, it changes nothing about what Revelation says these two will do or the authority given to them to do it.

## Judah, Israel, and the Path Into Exile

The historical backdrop behind Ezekiel's scroll — and behind the instruction to "submit and endure" that this chapter draws from it — runs through two related but distinct national catastrophes.

The northern kingdom of Israel, the ten tribes, spent roughly two centuries drifting further from covenant faithfulness after the kingdom split following Solomon's death. When the Assyrian Empire began pressing westward in the eighth century BC, Israel did not turn back to the Lord. Assyria conquered Samaria in 722 BC and deported the population, scattering the northern tribes in a way from which they never returned as a distinct national body — the origin of what later tradition would call the "lost tribes."

Judah, the southern kingdom, faced its own confrontation with Assyria roughly two decades later, in 701 BC, when King Sennacherib's army surrounded Jerusalem under King Hezekiah. Hezekiah's response was the opposite of the north's: he humbled himself, sought the Lord, and — as 2 Kings 19 and Isaiah 37 both record — the angel of the Lord struck down the Assyrian camp in a single night, and Sennacherib withdrew without taking the city. Judah's survival at that moment was tied directly to the posture Hezekiah took toward the crisis, not to any advantage in military strength.

That reprieve did not produce lasting faithfulness. Roughly a century later, as the Babylonian Empire eclipsed Assyria, Judah faced the same choice again — and, under later kings such as Zedekiah, made the opposite decision Hezekiah had made. Jeremiah and Ezekiel both delivered the same divine counsel: submit to Babylon, go into exile peacefully, and it will go well. Zedekiah instead broke his sworn allegiance to Babylon and allied with Egypt, provoking Nebuchadnezzar II's final siege of Jerusalem, which ended in 586 BC with the city and the temple destroyed. Daniel and his companions, among those already taken to Babylon in an earlier deportation, are the biblical picture of what obedience to that same counsel looked like — not merely surviving exile, but being elevated within it.

The pattern across both crises is the same: the nations who submitted to what God said was coming were preserved or even prospered within it; the ones who resisted, on the conviction that resistance was faithfulness, brought about the very destruction they were trying to avoid. That is the specific historical pattern this chapter draws on when it applies Ezekiel's commission — deliver an unwelcome message to a people who will largely not receive it — to the church's posture toward the tribulation still to come.

## The Divine Self-Oath in Scripture

The picture of God swearing by himself appears at enough points in Scripture that it functions as a recognizable pattern rather than an isolated oddity. Hebrews 6:13–18 states the underlying logic most directly: an oath ordinarily works by invoking a power greater than the one making the promise, so that the greater power stands as guarantee if the promise is broken. Since there is no power greater than God, that mechanism is unavailable to him in the ordinary sense — so, per Hebrews, he takes on both roles: he is the one making the promise, and by swearing by himself he becomes his own guarantee.

The same move appears in the Song of Moses in Deuteronomy 32:40, where God says, "I lift my hand to heaven and declare: as surely as I live forever," immediately before pronouncing judgment on his enemies and vindication for his people — his own eternal life is the thing invoked to certify what follows. It appears again, more concretely, in the covenant-cutting ceremony of Genesis 15, where God alone passes as a smoking firepot and blazing torch between the divided animal halves while Abraham sleeps: God takes both sides of the oath himself, rather than allowing the covenant's certainty to rest even partly on Abraham's future faithfulness.

Revelation 10:5–7 places this same pattern in the mouth of the mighty angel — raising his right hand to heaven and swearing "by him who lives forever and ever" that there will be no more delay. Read against its background in Deuteronomy and Genesis, the oath is not evidence of a limitation on the one making it. It is the strongest possible form a divine promise can take, because the only thing backing it is the one thing in the universe that cannot fail to keep its word.
